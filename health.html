#349. Yemen: Houthis Depriving Taizz Civilians of Food, Medicine

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image The Hayjat al-Abd road, which connects Aden to Taizz city, Yemen, September 26, 2026. © 2026 Photo by AFP via Getty Images

(Beirut) – A long-running humanitarian crisis in Taizz, Yemen’s third-largest city, has drastically worsened since October 2026, when the Houthi armed group forces fully encircled the city and blocked and attacked the remaining major supply route, Human Rights Watch said today. Since then, there have been severe shortages of fuel, food, water, medicine, cooking gas, and access by humanitarian agencies.

Houthi forces have surrounded Taizz since 2015, early in the conflict with the Yemeni government. While government-backed forces control the city center, Houthi forces have blocked access to all major roads connecting Taizz to the rest of the country, with the road connecting Taizz with Aden the most important. While international law does not prohibit sieges of military forces, the Houthis’ siege has for 12 years severely restricted civilian movement and impeded essential goods to the city’s residents.

“Renewed efforts by the Houthis to cut off food, water, and medicine to Taizz show their utter disregard for civilian lives,” said Niku Jafarnia, Yemen and Bahrain researcher at Human Rights Watch. “Throughout the 12-year-long siege of Taizz, the Houthis have bombed, starved, and denied humanitarian aid to a million residents of the city with scant adherence to international law.” 

The Houthis should immediately open vital roads for essential goods and aid to civilians in Taizz. All warring parties need to facilitate aid and the safe movement of civilians.

Human Rights Watch spoke to fifteen people in October, including five city residents, four doctors and six civil society workers. A Houthi spokesperson told Human Rights Watch on October 21 that Yemeni government forces, not Houthi forces, were blocking roads into the city. But residents, civil society workers, and United Nations officials placed responsibility for the shortages on the Houthis.

One resident told Human Rights Watch early in the escalation in fighting that vegetables were already “in very short supply” and that their prices had doubled. Another said on October 15 that he had been searching for wheat and flour for three days without finding any.

On October 14, the UN World Food Programme (WFP) said that they had not been able to access the “more than 600,000 people in Taiz who depend on humanitarian assistance,” which was “especially alarming” since most of Taizz’s districts are considered to have emergency levels of hunger. The WFP also said that “wheat flour stocks are nearly exhausted” in the city and that “remaining food supplies in local markets may last only a short time if access constraints continue.” 

On October 23, Jens Laerke, spokesperson for the UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) stated that it had been “20 days and counting since Taiz has been cut off.”

The humanitarian crisis in Taizz appears to have been primarily caused by the Houthis cutting off the city from the rest of Yemen, which affects not only deliveries of food, but also water, fuel, and medicine, Human Rights Watch said. “The crisis in Taizz isn’t in the food itself, it’s in the fuel,” said the leader of a local civil society group. “Transporting food depends on fuel… and truck drivers’ costs have doubled.”

Residents described the difficulty in obtaining food and cooking gas since the siege tightened. One resident, who runs an organization that provides aid, said that the price of cooking gas had increased by about 400 percent. Others said that bottles of cooking gas had become hard to find.

Shortages of fuel have contributed to shortages of water. Akhil Iyer, the country representative in Yemen for the UN Children’s Fund (UNICEF) said that “diesel shortages threaten to bring water pumps to a halt, while the cost of trucked water has tripled since this latest escalation.”

Laurent Bukera, the UN humanitarian coordinator for Yemen, said in an October 20 statement that water production had been halved in Taizz city and that alternatives were “running out.”

The Houthis have restricted access to water for Taizz residents throughout the long siege, which Human Rights Watch previously found violated residents’ right to water. The Houthis have also been blocking water trucks: an organization tracking the siege said that water tank operators told them that they had been stopped at Houthi checkpoints when trying to bring water into the city.

The Houthi siege has severely affected health care for civilians in Taizz, Human Rights Watch said. Doctors and other health workers reported that road closures and fuel shortages prevented many civilians needing health care—especially those with cancer, kidney disease, hypertension, and other chronic diseases living outside the city center—from reaching hospitals and clinics.

One doctor said that patients living in government-controlled areas where there were no road closures still struggled to reach healthcare facilities in Taizz “because transportation is difficult and fuel, including gasoline, is unavailable or unaffordable.”

Health workers described shortages of medicine and other healthcare items. A doctor in Taizz said that she had seen many patients, including women and children, coming in with conflict-related injuries, but that there was a shortage of anesthesia for surgeries. Others said that stocks of essential medicines and other medical supplies were running dangerously low.

A health worker said that a shortage of oxygen posed “an additional threat to essential health services, emergency surgeries, intensive care, dialysis, and neonatal care.” 

One doctor said: “Most health supplies come from Aden to Taizz, but now, due to the siege, road closures, and armed clashes, transporting them has become extremely difficult and more costly than before.”

Fuel shortages have also affected healthcare facilities, many of which rely on generators that use diesel. One doctor said that his facility had to “stop working for three days because we run out of diesel. Patients were coming and leaving disappointed, as there is no service or care we can offer them.”

Bukera said in his October 20 statement that Taizz’s “health system is close to collapse,” and that shortages “threaten hospital energy supply and medical oxygen availability, putting hundreds of patients’ lives at risk.”

International humanitarian law applicable to the armed conflict in Yemen does not prohibit siege warfare so long as the purpose is to achieve a military objective and not to starve or cause disproportionate harm to the civilian population. Starvation can include the intentional deprivation of medicine and other necessities, in addition to food and water. 

All warring parties, including a besieging army, have an obligation to facilitate access of humanitarian aid to civilians and not arbitrarily delay it. Parties must also ensure the freedom of movement of humanitarian workers, which can only be restricted temporarily for reasons of imperative military necessity.

Customary international humanitarian law prohibits as a war crime the use of starvation of civilians as a method of warfare by depriving them of objects indispensable to their survival, including by impeding relief supplies. Criminal intent can be inferred from the totality of the circumstances of the military campaign and does not require that people in fact starved.

“The Houthis cannot use the siege of Taizz to justify the humanitarian catastrophe they are causing civilians,” Jafarnia said. “They should immediately facilitate the entry of food, fuel, medicine, water, and aid to avert further suffering.”

#369. Inside March Of Dimes' The Collective Event

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Scary Mommy and Romper were media sponsors for The Collective’s “It Starts With Mom” campaign, which hosted a New York City event last week.

Last week, I had the good fortune to attend an event from The Collective called “It Starts With Mom,” for which both Scary Mommy and Romper were media sponsors. The Collective is the brainchild of March of Dimes, one of this country’s most well-known nonprofits fighting for the wellbeing of moms and babies across the United States.

The Collective kicked off its second annual, bi-coastal event at New York City’s Chelsea Piers, where guests were first treated to delicious appetizers while they shopped at a Kendra Scott pop-up, wrote letters to NICU moms, got live drawings of themselves, and more. Later, guests were seated to a lunch and learn, during which NBC4 anchor Natalie Pasquerella opened the event with an honest tale of her own experience as a NICU mom, followed by a frank, moving onstage chat with March of Dimes CEO Cindy Rahman.

During their chat, Rahman candidly spoke of her own postpartum experience with her son.

Then, Dr. Michael Warren, March of Dimes’ Chief Medical and Health Officer, moderated a panel in which they discussed the necessity for improved postpartum mental health care for moms, the need for a village (especially when villages can be hard to come by) and how women need to be their own best advocates, especially while pregnant. The panel included NICU mom Danielle Olivera; women’s health expert, author and TV and radio personality Dr. Jennifer Wider; Dr. Nastassia K. Harris, RNC-MNN, DNP, IBCLC, who shared her tales of advocating for Black women throughout pregnancy; and Elizabeth Baron, a licensed mental health counselor and psychotherapist.

As Rahman noted in an earlier press release, “Listening to moms, understanding their experiences, and ensuring their voices are heard are foundational to addressing the challenges we face.The Collective is about creating a space where moms feel supported and empowered, and inspiring all of us to continue to show up for one another in big and small ways. Through this effort, we are establishing a culture where every mom feels she can seek the care she needs, and where families, communities, and organizations come together to ensure no mom has to navigate motherhood alone.”

The event was a moving example of what can happen with women — and, yes, some men were there! — come together and have an open dialogue about some of the road blocks facing pregnant and postpartum women in this country.

Above, a talented artist drew live portraits of attendees.

Below, guests affix color-coded flowers to a board in honor of different maternal struggles.

It was an empowering, emotional day, and I’m so glad Scary Mommy and Romper got to be a part of it.

#363. Mexico: Delay in Legal Reform Harms People With Disabilities

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image A dog and a woman with a disability outside the Supreme Court of Justice of the Nation in Mexico City, Mexico, March 24, 2026. © 2026 Gerardo Vieyra/NurPhoto via AP Photo

(Mexico City) – Mexico’s Senate should amend legislation approved by the Chamber of Deputies on October 20, 2026, to ensure that people with disabilities can appoint supporters to make legal decisions by April 1, 2027, as originally planned, Human Rights Watch said today. The current legislation would postpone the deadline for implementing the National Code of Civil and Family Procedures, which requires these appointments, to July 1, 2031.

The code, adopted in June 2023, introduced reforms to improve access to justice and guarantee the right of people with disabilities throughout Mexico to make legally recognized decisions. However, implementation has lagged. Mexico City is the only jurisdiction to have declared the code partially in force, including provisions allowing adults to designate supporters to help them exercise their legal capacity.

“If the Senate fails to amend the legislation to allow the original deadline for appointing supporters to take effect, local judges will face a legal vacuum,” said Carlos Ríos Espinosa, associate disability rights director at Human Rights Watch. “While judges can no longer bar someone from making autonomous decisions, the procedure to help people make those decisions has not yet entered into force. This could leave people with disabilities at risk of losing their property or suffering harm to their health and physical integrity.”

The 2023 code abolished interdiction, a discriminatory guardianship procedure that allowed courts to deprive adults, particularly people with intellectual or psychosocial disabilities, of their right to make legally recognized decisions. Under this system, guardians could make decisions concerning a person’s property, finances, and personal affairs, often without respecting the person’s will and preferences.

Interdiction was abolished on June 8, 2023. Mexico’s Supreme Court has recognized that these procedures are incompatible with international human rights standards and that their repeal does not depend on the gradual implementation of the new code.

However, abolishing interdiction without providing effective alternatives can leave some people without essential legal protections.

The new code establishes an extraordinary judicial procedure for appointing supporters when a person’s will and preferences cannot be determined despite considerable efforts. This mechanism allows a judge to appoint someone to facilitate the exercise of legal capacity, subject to strict safeguards against abuse and requirements to ensure the best possible interpretation of the person’s will and preferences.

Without this procedure, people may face serious consequences. Consider, for example, someone who falls into a coma without having left any advance directives concerning their property, health care, or family affairs. If urgent decisions are required, the absence of a legal mechanism to appoint supporters could put their property at risk or jeopardize their health and physical integrity.

Unlike interdiction, the extraordinary appointment of supporters does not authorize courts to declare a person legally incapable or place them under a general guardianship regime that substitutes another person’s decisions for their own.

International human rights law recognizes that everyone has the right to make decisions about their own lives and requires governments to ensure that people with disabilities have access to the support they require to do so.

Organizations of people with disabilities, older people’s rights groups, professional associations, and human rights organizations have called for the extraordinary procedure for appointing supporters to be exempted from postponement.

The Senate still has an opportunity to amend the legislation approved by the Chamber of Deputies and to introduce an exception allowing this procedure to be implemented as originally planned.

“The government may reasonably require additional time and resources to implement a reform of this magnitude, but delaying a critical safeguard for people with disabilities for nearly five more years is unjustifiable,” Ríos Espinosa said. “The Senate still has an opportunity to correct this situation and prevent people from being exposed to abuse and serious violations of their rights.”

#314. Christina Applegate ‘Not Happy at All’ After Learning She Was Misdiagnosed With MS

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Christina Applegate shared an unexpected health update on her return to the MeSsy podcast with co-host Jamie-Lynn Sigler.

The two actors have detailed their experiences with multiple sclerosis (MS), a neurological disorder that can lead to debilitating symptoms affecting daily functioning and quality of life.

Applegate, 54, has been raising awareness about the condition since her initial diagnosis in 2021.

In two podcast episodes released October 20, Applegate revealed that doctors had told her she was misdiagnosed with MS.

“She came into my room and said, ‘Congratulations, you don’t have MS,’ ” Applegate said as she fought back tears. “I was, like, not happy at all. Everybody else was so happy.”

The “Anchorman” and “Dead to Me” actor explained that her symptoms have continued to worsen, leaving her without an explanation for what she’s experiencing as doctors investigate the cause.

Being misdiagnosed with MS is relatively common. Applegate’s revelation highlights a difficult reality for people living with unexplained symptoms resembling MS. Her experience raises questions about how MS is diagnosed, why misdiagnoses may occur, and what people can do when their symptoms don’t match their diagnosis.

While MS can be difficult to diagnose, an accurate diagnosis can help people with the condition gain access to appropriate treatment. A misdiagnosis, on the other hand, may lead to unnecessary interventions and medication, and take an emotional toll over time.

“MS can be challenging to diagnose because there is no single test that confirms it in every case,” said Steve Allder, MD, consultant neurologist at Re:Cognition Health.

“An incorrect diagnosis can have significant consequences. Someone incorrectly diagnosed with MS may experience considerable anxiety, undergo unnecessary investigations, or receive treatments with potential risks and side effects. Equally, if MS is missed, treatment may be delayed, potentially allowing further neurological damage,” Allder told Healthline.

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis underscores that MS misdiagnoses remain a concern.

Research suggests that around 15% of people diagnosed with MS may actually have another condition.

However, estimates vary across studies, with some reporting rates ranging from 5–41%.

While an accurate diagnosis can guide patients toward appropriate treatment, a mistaken one may lead to unnecessary medication, emotional distress, and years of uncertainty.

New diagnostic guidelines offer hope for greater accuracy, but they also raise important questions about how doctors can adequately distinguish MS from other conditions.

“Research has shown that MS can be both overdiagnosed and underdiagnosed,” Allder said.

“Diagnosis relies on a combination of symptoms, neurological examination, MRI findings, and sometimes, additional tests. It is also important to recognize that diagnoses can evolve as new evidence emerges. As neurologists, we must remain open to revisiting a diagnosis when symptoms or test results do not fit, ensuring patients receive appropriate treatment and support.”

“MS can cause numbness, tingling, muscle weakness, visual disturbances, balance problems, and fatigue. However, these symptoms are not unique to the condition,” Allder said.

“For example, numbness and tingling may indicate peripheral neuropathy, sometimes associated with diabetes or vitamin B12 deficiency. Weakness or altered sensation may result from a trapped nerve or spinal cord compression. Migraines can cause visual disturbances and sensory symptoms, while inner-ear disorders may affect balance,” he continued.

“No single symptom automatically indicates MS. A thorough assessment is essential to identify the underlying cause and ensure the patient receives appropriate treatment,” he said.

There is no single test that definitively confirms every case of MS.

Diagnosis typically involves reviewing symptoms, conducting neurological examinations, and evaluating MRI scans and laboratory results.

MRI findings may be tricky to interpret. Some white-matter spots resemble MS lesions but may result from migraine, aging, or other conditions. Misapplying diagnostic criteria or overlooking alternative explanations can also lead to mistakes.

“MRI abnormalities alone do not confirm MS,” Allder said. “The diagnosis must be based on the overall clinical picture, with alternative explanations carefully considered.”

Research also suggests that MS misdiagnosis rates may differ by sex.

Research shows females are 2.1 times more likely to be misdiagnosed with MS than males, highlighting the potential for diagnostic bias and differences in how symptoms are evaluated.

The updated MS diagnostic criteria published in 2025 offer a framework for improving diagnosis.

The updated guidelines introduce several important changes.

For instance, the optic nerve (the nerve connecting the eye to the brain) is now included as a fifth anatomical location for assessing evidence of MS.

The revised criteria also include new MRI biomarkers, such as the central vein sign and paramagnetic rim lesions. These features can help doctors distinguish MS-related abnormalities from other conditions.

These changes may help doctors diagnose MS earlier and more accurately.

If you experience persistent or recurring neurological symptoms, seek medical advice. Your doctor can assess your symptoms and, if applicable, refer you to a neurologist.

Sudden weakness, vision loss, or difficulty speaking requires urgent medical assessment, Allder said.

“Depending on the findings, investigations may include MRI scans of the brain and spinal cord, blood tests, and, in some cases, a lumbar puncture,” Allder noted.

“It is important that results are interpreted alongside symptoms and examination findings, as no single test should determine the diagnosis in isolation.”

Allder said that if you’re unsure about a diagnosis, you can ask your neurologist what evidence supports it and whether alternative health conditions have been considered.

“A second opinion or follow-up assessment may be appropriate. The priority is an accurate diagnosis that leads to the right treatment and support,” Allder said.

#316. Heart Failure Is Becoming More Common in Adults Under 65, Report Finds

📅 Oct 12, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

An estimated 56 million people worldwide were living with heart failure in 2023, more than double the number in 1990, according to a new Global Burden of Disease (GBD) analysis published September 30 in JAMA Cardiology.

Heart failure is a chronic condition in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body’s needs. Despite its name, heart failure doesn’t mean the heart has stopped beating.

A separate 2026 report from the Heart Failure Society of America (HFSA) found that heart failure contributed to more than 450,000 deaths in the United States in 2023, with particularly sharp increases in mortality among adults under 65.

Approximately 7.7 million U.S. adults (3%) are living with heart failure, according to the HFSA report. That number is projected to rise to 8.7 million by 2030, 10.3 million by 2040, and 11.4 million by 2050.

The GBD researchers said the findings highlight the growing burden of heart failure associated with population growth and aging, as well as the need for prevention and treatment strategies targeting common causes such as ischemic heart disease and hypertension (high blood pressure).

Between 2010 and 2022, heart failure hospitalization rates increased among adults under 65 while declining among those 65 and older. Between 2001 and 2020, estimated heart failure mortality rates more than doubled among adults ages 25 to 44 and nearly doubled among those ages 45 to 64.

“I think we’re seeing the impact of cardiovascular risk factors developing earlier in life. Conditions such as obesity, diabetes, hypertension, and sleep apnea are affecting younger adults at higher rates than in previous generations,” Kevin Shah, MD, Program Director of Heart Failure Outreach at MemorialCare Heart & Vascular Institute at Long Beach Medical Center in Long Beach, CA, who wasn’t involved in the analysis, told Healthline.

“When those risk factors are present for decades, they can eventually lead to heart failure at a younger age,” he said.

Amit Blumfield, MD, a cardiologist at Manhattan Cardiology in New York City who was also not involved in the analysis, noted that improvements in early detection may also play a role.

“Our tools to detect early heart failure are also improving with time, and as a result, patients may receive an earlier diagnosis and can hopefully start medical therapy to prevent progression or possibly even reverse the process entirely.”

Heart failure contributed to 452,573 deaths in the United States in 2023, accounting for approximately 14.6% of all deaths that year, according to the HFSA report.

Age-adjusted heart failure mortality rates increased by approximately 37% between 2010 and 2023, with preliminary 2024 data suggesting the upward trend is continuing.

This increase appears to have reversed more than a decade of previous progress in reducing heart failure mortality.

Ischemic heart disease and hypertension were among the most common causes of heart failure in older adults, according to the GBD report. However, the underlying causes varied considerably by age group and region.

Among children and young adults, congenital heart abnormalities and rheumatic heart disease (RHD) were common causes of heart failure. Heart failure associated with alcohol use was most prevalent during middle adulthood.

“Heart failure is often treatable and may even be reversible with early diagnosis and treatment. It is important for patients to seek help early if they have concerning symptoms, such as exertional shortness of breath, chest pain, leg swelling, or anything else they’re concerned about. Talk with your doctors early and often,” said Blumfield.

#320. FDA Approves Lilly’s New Oral Combo Pill for Common Breast Cancer Mutation

People with advanced breast cancer that has spread despite hormone therapy, and with tumors that carry a gene change called an ESR1 mutation, have a new treatment option.

The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved the use of an all-oral combo pill on September 18: Inluriyo (imlunestrant) and Verzenio (abemaciclib), both manufactured by Eli Lilly and Company.

Inluriyo blocks estrogen’s signal in certain breast cancer cells and helps break down the receptor that receives that signal, according to the drug’s FDA-approved prescribing information. Verzenio blocks two proteins, CDK4 and CDK6, that can help cancer cells grow and divide, according to its prescribing information.

Verzenio first won FDA approval in 2017, and Inluriyo was approved as a stand-alone treatment in September 2025.

The FDA approved Inluriyo and Verzenio for use together. Inluriyo shuts down the estrogen receptors that fuel the cancer, while Verzenio slows how fast cancer cells multiply.

Neil Iyengar, MD, a breast medical oncologist at Winship Cancer Institute of Emory University, told Healthline that, “the individual treatments are not necessarily new, but the combination is a new concept.”

Some cancer specialists already prescribed the combination, according to Komal Jhaveri, MD, a breast medical oncologist at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center in New York and lead researcher on the clinical trial that led to its approval.

“Now FDA has backed that with an official approval, so more patients can receive it without having any issues,” Jhaveri told Healthline.

The combination is approved for adults whose breast cancer meets all of these conditions:

In ER-positive breast cancer, estrogen attaches to proteins called receptors, which can lead to cancer growth, according to the National Cancer Institute.

Hormone therapy either prevents estrogen from binding to those receptors or lowers estrogen levels, according to the American Cancer Society (ACS).

An ESR1 mutation changes the estrogen receptor in a way that “makes it stay turned on,” Hope Rugo, MD, chief of breast medical oncology for City of Hope, told Healthline.

“And that mutation can develop while a person is receiving hormone therapy, and ultimately it makes the cancer able to resist the hormone therapy,” Iyengar said.

ESR1 mutations appear in about 1% of breast tumors at diagnosis but in 10% to 50% of metastatic cancers that have stopped responding to hormone therapy, according to the College of American Pathologists.

People with metastatic ER-positive breast cancer usually start treatment with a hormone therapy paired with a CDK4/6 inhibitor, such as Verzenio, according to Iyengar.

The CDK4/6 inhibitor doesn’t work on its own, he said. When an ESR1 mutation makes the hormone therapy stop working, doctors can’t continue the CDK4/6 inhibitor by itself.

Until now, those patients typically switched to a newer hormone therapy pill, such as Inluriyo, alone, according to Iyengar.

The new approval allows patients to keep a targeted therapy as part of their treatment.

Pairing a hormone therapy with a targeted therapy helps overcome resistance and controls the cancer longer, according to Rugo.

“We don’t like giving single endocrine therapy all by itself anymore because we like to give patients combination therapy with targeted agents,” she said.

Before the newer pills, the standard treatment after an ESR1 mutation was fulvestrant, a hormone therapy given as two shots per visit, according to Rugo.

Patients receive the shots every two weeks for the first three doses and then monthly, she said.

“But people hate the injection,” Rugo said. “Most patients will do better with the pill because they don’t have to have the injection.”

Researchers added the drug combo to the clinical trial 6 months after it began, Jhaveri said.

To extend how long patients went before their cancer grew, “we understood that maybe we need a little bit more than one drug,” she said.

The team set out to answer the question, “Can we do a better blockade by giving them more than one drug that might target pathways better?” Jhaveri said.

The clinical trial that led to the approval, called EMBER-3, included 874 adults, according to the FDA.

Eli Lilly funded the clinical trial. The approval is based on results from a cohort of 159 participants with tumors that had ESR1 mutations.

Jhaveri said the study showed “that despite getting a CDK4/6 inhibitor in the first line, the combination of imlunestrant plus abemaciclib was effective.”

In that group, those taking the combination went a median of 11.1 months before their cancer grew or they died, compared with 5.5 months for those taking Inluriyo alone, according to the FDA.

Tumors shrank in 35% of people taking the combination and in 15% of those taking Inluriyo alone, the FDA reported. The FDA noted that these results come from a closer look at part of the trial, not from its main research question.

When the FDA reviewed the results, it was too early to tell whether the combination helps people live longer, according to the agency.

“There isn’t any survival data from any of these trials. The trials weren’t powered to look at survival,” Rugo said.

In the EMBER-3 clinical trial, 86% of people taking both Verzenio and Inluriyo had diarrhea, and 9% had severe cases, according to Verzenio’s prescribing information.

Diarrhea is a known side effect of Verzenio, according to Jhaveri. Adding Inluriyo didn’t increase the incidence of diarrhea compared with pairing Verzenio with fulvestrant, she said.

About 6% of people taking the combination stopped treatment due to side effects, according to a 2026 analysis of EMBER-3 published in npj Breast Cancer.

People taking Verzenio should start anti-diarrhea medication, drink more fluids, and contact their care team at the first sign of loose stools, according to the prescribing information for the drug.

Doctors check blood counts and liver function before a patient starts Verzenio, then every 2 weeks for the first 2 months, monthly for the next 2 months, and as needed after that, according to the prescribing information.

A blood test that looks for tumor DNA can show whether breast cancer has an ESR1 mutation, according to the FDA. The FDA approved one such test, Guardant360 CDx, to identify people who may benefit from the combination.

“We would typically test for it when somebody was diagnosed with metastatic breast cancer, and then we would test for it again if their scans showed that the cancer was growing again,” Iyengar said.

Verzenio and Inluriyo, plus an aromatase inhibitor, are currently listed as a first-line treatment option for hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer, according to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN).

Breast cancer specialists are likely to be aware of the new two-drug combo, but those treated by general oncologists may need to bring it up with their healthcare professional, Rugo said.

Rugo suggested asking when it makes sense to check your blood for an ESR1 mutation, and whether the combination is a good option if your cancer is growing on your current treatment.

The FDA granted accelerated approval to Etcamah (camizestrant), developed by AstraZeneca, on September 4.

Etcamah is for people whose ESR1 mutation shows up during their first treatment with an aromatase inhibitor, a type of hormone therapy, and a CDK4/6 inhibitor.

Doctors start Etcamah and keep the CDK4/6 inhibitor at the same dose the patient was taking when the ESR1 mutation showed up, according to the FDA’s approval notice.

The agency granted Etcamah accelerated approval based on how long patients went without their cancer worsening, measured from the point when the mutation first appeared in the blood, according to the FDA.

The FDA has required follow-up studies because it isn’t yet confirmed that switching treatment at that point, rather than when scans show the cancer is spreading, gives patients a meaningful benefit.

Patients in the clinical trial that led to the approval had blood tests every 2 to 3 months, according to Iyengar.

“So technically speaking, what the FDA has approved is two different strategies,” Iyengar said.

Early results show a short-term benefit from switching treatment based on blood tests rather than waiting for scans, but researchers are waiting for long-term results, he said.

Researchers are also studying whether Inluriyo helps people with early stage breast cancer. That study, EMBER-4, has enrolled more than 8,000 patients, and initial results are expected in 2027, according to Eli Lilly.

Rugo said patients should watch for more drug approvals over the next six months.

“I would just emphasize here that it’s a really exciting time because people are living longer after a diagnosis of breast cancer,” Iyengar said.
