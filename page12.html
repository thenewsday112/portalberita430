#403. Joyce Wang Studio Returns to Mandarin Oriental The Landmark a Decade Later

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

More than a decade after first reimagining Mandarin Oriental The Landmark in Hong Kong, Joyce Wang Studio has renovated the hotel a second time. The new work covers its 109 rooms and suites, the mansion foyer, the spa, and the former MO Bar.

Joyce Wang, the studio’s principal, says the latest brief placed greater emphasis on the hotel’s connection to Hong Kong. “We took inspiration from the colonial country manors frequented by the Tai Kwuns of a bygone era to celebrate the beauty of these historic homes that still fascinate today,” she says. “Our vision this time has a greater focus on local relevance and the heritage of the building.”

The redesign is organized around the idea of a lived-in mansion, a type with many historical examples in Hong Kong. Green and orange anchor the palette, drawing on the city’s lush gardens and the terracotta tones of its colonial architecture. In the lobby and arrival sequence, classic architectural gestures meet contemporary detailing in stone, timber, textured wallcoverings, and custom eglomisé glass. An undulating staircase, artwork bays, and sculptural plinths guide guests through an arrival sequence modeled on that of a grand residence.

The city’s skyline runs through the building as a motif. The studio created custom artworks of the Hong Kong silhouette and produced them as carpets, tapestries, and eglomisé glass for the lobby, the elevators, and the guestroom corridors. In the rooms, custom rugs take their pattern from Hong Kong’s historic brickwork. They sit on lightened timber floors alongside cracked gesso wall finishes and a Fromental wallcovering incorporating hand-painted and silk elements.

Art was integrated into the design program from the start. Wang says the artwork program “is rigorous in that there is a story behind each piece.” The collection brings together work by emerging and established artists, both local and international. Wang describes the hotel as a pied-à-terre for guests to experience the city up close. Sculptural vases, artisanal vessels, and other objects were selected to look like things gathered over time in a private home.

Together, these details give the hotel the layered character of a private residence, while the references to Hong Kong’s architecture, landscape, and artistic community keep the design firmly rooted in its setting. Rather than simply revisiting the studio’s earlier work, the renovation offers a more distinctly local interpretation of the luxury hotel.

#24. Japanese prosecutors charge US marine suspected of robbing and killing woman

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Devin Jacob Ballard, 20, denies charges that he fatally strangled Anna Yagi, 39, in a hotel on the island of Okinawa

Prosecutors in Japan have filed formal charges against a US marine suspected of murdering and robbing a woman while stationed on the country’s island of Okinawa, according to Japanese media.

Devin Jacob Ballard, a 20-year-old lance corporal assigned to Marine Corps Air Station Futenma in Okinawa, is charged with fatally strangling Anna Yagi, 39, at a hotel in the city and stealing from her a backpack containing a wallet, the public broadcaster NHK reported.

Ballard had initially been arrested in Yagi’s killing on 4 October, about a day after she was found dead in Naha, Okinawa’s capital. Prosecutors indicted him in the case on Sunday, and he has reportedly denied the charges.

NHK and other Japanese media outlets had previously reported that Ballard was arrested after surveillance camera footage from near the scene of Yagi’s killing showed a man entering a room with a woman and later leaving the scene with her belongings.

Ballard evidently matched the description of the suspect seen in the footage, was confronted and agreed to accompany investigators voluntarily to a local police station, Japanese media reported.

While being questioned by police, Ballard denied having a hand in Yagi’s killing, saying, “I don’t know anything about it,” NHK reported. But during subsequent questioning Ballard was alleged to have “made statements that partially hinted at his involvement”, the broadcaster reported.

A statement reported by NHK and reported to Yagi’s family on Sunday expressed a hope that Ballard “will confront what he has done and make amends commensurate with what he has done”.

“She lost her life at the young age of 39,” the statement from Yagi’s family added. “She … wanted to live much, much longer.”

Yagi’s killing prompted protests in Okinawa and the Japanese capital of Tokyo, with political leaders publicly condemning her killing. It also triggered a curfew and alcohol ban for US military members stationed in Okinawa, where most of the 54,000 US troops in Japan are based.

Crimes committed by US troops in Japan – including rape, assault, hit-and-run and drunk-driving – have long strained relations between the countries, which are close military allies.

A Japanese court in June 2025 sentenced US marine Jamel Clayton, then 22, to seven years in prison after he was convicted of choking and sexually assaulting a woman in her 20s.

In 2017, a former US marine and civilian military base worker named Kenneth Shinzato was sentenced to life in prison for the rape and murder of a local Okinawa woman.

US military forces in Japan that same year banned all personnel from consuming alcohol after an intoxicated US marine crashed his vehicle into a mini-truck, killing a local driver, age 61.

The Yomiuri Shimbun newspaper reported that about 6,000 US military personnel have been arrested since the US returned Okinawa to Japan after the second world war in 1972.

There were 80 such arrests in 2024, constituting a 20-year high.

After Ballard’s arrest, Shinjiro Koizumi, Japan’s defense minister, said: “Incidents and accidents involving US military personnel cause great anxiety among the local community and must not be allowed to happen.”

#95. Man United fans see their owners for what they are. They see their greed and their acquisitiveness. Man City fans are still in denial about theirs: OLIVER HOLT

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Published: 12:22 GMT, 26 October 2026 | Updated: 17:52 GMT, 27 October 2026

It is usual, in days of disillusion and crisis at a football club, when results are bad and the league position is heading south fast, for the manager to be the lightning conductor for dissent and anger. It is he who takes the brickbats and suffers the abuse and listens to the chants demanding that he give up the fight.

‘We hope you die soon,’ the front cover of a Southampton fanzine shouted at the club’s then manager, Ian Branfoot, in 1993. And if that is extreme, the sight of planes flying above grounds with a banner proclaiming ‘WengerOut’, or similar, has long become part of the supporters’ armoury of resistance.

Not on Sunday. Not at Old Trafford. After Manchester United lost 3-0 at home to Bournemouth and sank to 16th in the Premier League table, the home fans did not turn on Michael Carrick. They turned on the players but most of all, they turned on the club’s hated owners, the Glazers and Sir Jim Ratcliffe.

They turned on them because they know that the owners, much more than Carrick, are responsible for the depths to which a once great club has sunk, both on and off the pitch. They turned on them because the debt they saddled the club with when they bought it in 2005 has now grown to over £1.15billion.

They turned on them because that debt has cost United £852million in interest payments. In the last financial year, the interest payment was £37m, an increase from £34m the previous year. They turned on them because the club has declared war on its own season-ticket holders in its bid to increase the number of tourists and day-trippers spending money at Old Trafford.

At various points during the match, fans were given updates about the plans for a new stadium but those were met with derision, too. Many loyal United fans think the new stadium, and the prices they will be asked to pay to sit in it, are just another way of driving them out of the club so they can be replaced by shinier, happier people with bigger wallets.

United fans see their owners for what they are. They see their greed and their acquisitiveness. They see through the homilies about how much the fans mean to the club.

We know what you are, they have been saying to them for many years. They have said it with protests and with banners. They said it on Sunday with chants and anger.

These are dark days for Manchesterism in football. If United fans have seen through their owners, City fans, who are trying to come to terms with the fact that their owners have been found guilty of cheating on an industrial scale between 2009 and 2018, are not quite there yet. Maybe one day, when the legal process has played out fully, they will feel it, too.

Many are still in denial about what the emirate of Abu Dhabi has done to their club. That is understandable. Abu Dhabi has pumped money into City, not taken it out.

Abu Dhabi has overseen an incredible period of success in east Manchester, winning the league title an unprecedented four times in succession, compared to United’s failures.

But the reality for both sets of supporters is stark. United have been hijacked by corporate leeches whose model is said by financial experts to be the ‘aggressive utilisation of debt financing secured against high-value, cash-generative assets to fund acquisition and expansion’. United are the Glazers’ cash-cow.

City have become a plaything for a petro-state. They have become one of the world’s most high-profile sportswashing projects and one of the most effective, as Abu Dhabi has sought to sluice away the reputation of its repressive regime with the beautiful football of Pep Guardiola and the series of golden triumphs he delivered.

For now, Abu Dhabi brief about ‘irrefutable evidence’ they possess in their pursuit for justice from the Premier League. They are said to be ‘bullish’ about their chances of success in their appeal.

The idea, propagated brilliantly by some Abu Dhabi acolytes, that the modern City are somehow a champion of the oppressed in the Premier League, fighting against the tyranny of some imagined ‘red cartel’, might be a clever ruse but it is also laughably ridiculous.

Abu Dhabi’s defenders want to couch this battle as Harry Potter versus the Dark Lord but even a fool can see it’s not like that. City want dominance. They are not here for altruism.

They are not here to spread the love. And they have achieved dominance. Their four successive titles earlier in this decade was a level of rule that no club has ever achieved in the long history of the English game.

Do you really think Aston Villa would have a chance of winning the league in seasons to come? Or Everton? Or Spurs? No chance of that. None at all.

The imagined ‘red cartel’ would be replaced by a very real nation state cartel. Abu Dhabi (Manchester City) or Saudi Arabia (Newcastle United) would win the Premier League every season. No one else could get remotely close to the level of spending facilitated by their public investment funds. The Premier League would be finished as a spectacle.

City wanted to join the European Super League, don’t forget. City, under Abu Dhabi, are not anti-establishment. They are the establishment. They have stripped ambitious, proud mid-table clubs like Everton and Villa of their best players time and again over the last 20 years. Red cartel? Please. Do me a favour.

#20. US forces accused of ‘weaponising’ Somalia’s floods by trapping women and children in the path of El Niño

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Exclusive: Coordinated airstrikes on territory held by al-Shabaab are driving the civilian population to find refuge alongside a river where rising water levels are a threat to safety. Is it a deliberate military strategy?

The water was rising rapidly. It had already reached Cayni Ali’s waist. Her home, five metres from the flooded River Juba in southern Somalia, never stood a chance. There was nowhere to go. Ali’s city of Jilib was under attack. Repeated airstrikes, said the mother of six, were pinning its population beside the rising river.

“The drones come all the time,” said the 27-year-old. “We hear them constantly. They kill animals, civilians. The town is big but destruction is everywhere.”

Asho Omar from Malenda, a village near Jilib, described “constant” airstrikes that had forced people towards the banks of the Juba.

“The airstrikes are everywhere; it is difficult to move. So many people have been killed.”

Their testimony offers a rare glimpse of life inside the stronghold of al-Shabaab, the Islamist militants who control 30% of Somalia. Closed off from the outside world, residents describe a city dealing with significant civilian casualties. Bodies, some said, lay in the streets.

The ongoing conflict is a legally ambiguous, shadowy war coordinated by the United States in east Africa, in which there is little accountability for harm to Somali civilians.

The United States Africa Command (Africom) would not share “operational security” details of a seemingly accelerating campaign that has also raised questions over potential covert US strikes. But there is, perhaps, an even darker dimension to the offensive on al-Shabaab’s de facto capital.

Sources say the military strategy appears to be seeking to drown those living in al-Shabaab territory by “weaponising” a rapidly strengthening El Niño, forecast to intensify Somalia’s rainy season from this month.

“The airstrikes are forcing people to go close to the river. It’s an extremely aggressive offensive, exploiting El Niño as a weapon of war to push the population into flood-prone areas,” said a source monitoring the Juba floodplain in southern Somalia.

Pinning the population – which includes large numbers of women and children – on the river’s floodplain has raised concerns of large-scale deaths in the months ahead.

“The official line is that they are targeting military positions and al-Shabaab, but because al-Shabaab is embedded within the community, it’s difficult to avoid collateral damage,” added the source.

When El Niño last struck Somalia in 2023, it caused the worst floods seen in the country for a century, displacing more than 1.2 million people. Analysts have questioned the ethics of the US military allegedly using mapping data from flood-prone areas – conceived to mitigate civilian casualties – to inflict harm.

Jethro Norman, a senior researcher with the Danish Institute for International Studies, said: “A weaponisation of El Niño is a reversal of process – using humanitarian remote sensing to inflict casualties rather than to mitigate harm. You capture the cumulative impact of a sustained airstrikes campaign in an El Niño flooding context.”

US officials deny exploiting climatic factors. A spokesperson said: “Africom does not target civilians, conduct operations for the purpose of displacing civilian populations or use weather or flooding as a method of warfare. Africom targets lawful military objectives and takes feasible measures to mitigate civilian harm.”

Nevertheless, many wonder if the opportunity to weaken al-Shabaab using the weather was too tempting to ignore, offering a catastrophic drowning event blamed on a supercharged El Niño.

Like many cities, Jilib’s location has been found wanting in an era of extreme weather. On a low-lying plain, the city is chronically vulnerable to flooding. In 2023, vast swathes of farmland were submerged. Halimo Abdikadir from Kamsuuma, south of Jillib, described the destruction of her home, 6 miles (10km) from the river. “The waters came quickly at night – when they left, everything was mud. A lot of animals drowned.”

Arbey Famu from Ahmed Yare, another village outside Jilib, expects this El Niño to be far worse than 2023: her crops of maize and sorghum are underwater.

“In Jilib, there is big flooding already,” said the 70-year-old.

US defence officials will know the early indicators of El Niño’s impact in al-Shabaab’s heartland. UN agencies say the Juba River basin is of “particular concern”. The International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), meanwhile, foresees “severe flooding”.

Attacking Jilib using ground forces carries much risk. Airstrikes can yield triumphs such as the death of a senior al-Shabaab commander on 1 October, but are unable to overrun a city of 100,000.

Ali Adan Ali, the commissioner for displaced people in Jubaland, is worried. In his Kismayo office, Ali identified 327 regions prone to flooding. By October, El Niño warnings had only been issued to half.

His chief concern is the lack of boats. Without them, he says, thousands in al-Shabaab territory risk being trapped on the east side of the river, unable to reach help in Kismayo, at the mouth of the Juba.

Three years ago, during the last El Niño, Ali says, 19,000 people were stranded for a week without food. “We are running out of time,” he says.

The absence of boats sums up Somalia’s funding woes. In 2023, the country’s aid budget was $2.2bn. Now it stands at about $300,000. Flood protection investment has dwindled.

Compounding concerns is al-Shabaab’s lack of preparedness in the area it controls. Ali has not received a response from the militants regarding El Niño readiness. Cayni Ali said there are no sandbags or flood defences deployed yet in Jilib. Earth walls were erected along the river but have proved insufficient.

“They are not big enough,” she said. The latest flood advisory for the River Juba urges communities to “reinforce” weak riverbanks.

Corroborating testimonies describe a prolonged – and seemingly indiscriminate – campaign of airstrikes against al-Shabaab that appears to have killed many civilians. The US would not share details of potential civilian fatalities “to protect US and partner forces and preserve operational security”.

Farhan Ahmed, who lived in Jilib until fleeing last month, described constant terror. “There were airstrikes every day, all the time.” One strike flattened five homes in his neighbourhood. Ahmed, 35, described bodies littering the city streets.

Fadumo Abdi claimed the airstrikes were largely failing to target militants. “The attacks are mostly killing civilians. Some were buried, but some were not,” she said.

Abdikadir, from Kamsuuma, added: “The drone attacks on Jilib are too much. So many people die.”

Yusuf Siidi, 27, from Araare near Jamame in al-Shabaab territory, witnessed three teenagers killed in one strike. Maryan Salah was at home near Jamame when a drone sent a shard of burning metal 20cm long into her back yard, landing next to her two-year-old daughter.

“It was so close. I am very angry. I’m not part of the conflict.”

Salah’s near miss predates the current offensive. She can recall only that it happened in “mid-March”. The US admits a drone strike near Jamame on 19 March, but no other data is provided.

Although eyewitness accounts detail an extraordinary number of strikes in Jilib, official US records confirm only one airstrike in Jilib during August. Six took place last month. Between the start of August and now, the US says it conducted a total of just 11 strikes.

Allegations of ambiguity increasingly characterise US operations in Somalia. It is an opaque approach that raises questions over whether Africom has authorised covert airstrikes in the region.

Records from the nearest hospital indicate intensive airstrikes or fighting. The ICRC has treated 205 patients with “weapon-related” injuries in Kismayo this year, a 43% increase on last year.

David Sterman, analyst at the New America thinktank, said: “The possibility that we are seeing covert US strikes always looms large when there are strikes without clear attribution.”

The US, however, said it publicised “all airstrikes conducted under its authority in Somalia. We do not maintain an undisclosed or unannounced strike count.”

Turkey has also used drones to target al-Shabaab in Somalia. Operating alongside Africom, Turkey rarely divulges any details of its strikes or military operations in Somalia.

Beyond airstrikes and floods, the primary concern for Somalis is hunger. Those fleeing the Juba valley – local authorities are offering humanitarian help to those who escape – say there is insufficient food to sustain them.

Omar’s family of 13 survives on one fish a day. How big is the fish? Omar held her hands less than 30cm apart.

Habiba Ali, 55, from Jamame, said her family – like many – shares one daily meal of maize. Time in between is spent hiding from conflict. “When the fighting stops, we wait for it start again.”

In Kismayo, too, long-term drought and USAID cuts have brought misery.

Shukri Ahmed, 32, the president of Galbeed refugee camp, estimates that 6,000 might have died in Kismayo’s network of displacement sites since the Trump administration abruptly shut USAID last year. Overnight, two children had died in makeshift shelters from diarrhoea.

El Niño will “deepen [Somalia’s] hunger levels”, warns the UN-backed body responsible for identifying famine.

Zakaria Mohammed, head nurse at Kismayo’s ICRC-supported hospital for five years, says children from al-Shabaab territory arrive every day so severely malnourished or weak they cannot be saved. Cases of child malnutrition rates have risen 40% this year.

Xaawo Nacdoor made it just in time. Al-Shabaab had blocked her leaving Jamame to receive treatment for her baby. But her 14-month-old daughter Faadumo was dying, so Nacdoor fled at night.

For three days, with just a bottle of water, Nacdoor walked with her daughter strapped to her back. On the second day, the toddler lost consciousness.

“She was severely dehydrated, but I kept going.” Arriving at the hospital, Faadumo weighed 4.1kg. Three days later: 4.4kg. She survived.

Most patients here vow never to return home, saying life under al-Shabaab meant no school, education, healthcare or food. For the thousands of families straddling the Juba River – often the poorest – things are about to get much worse.

#361. Sudan: One Year on from the Cataclysm of El Fasher

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image Displaced women and girls walk through the vast IDP site in Tawila, Sudan, February 23rd, 2026. © 2026 Giles Clarke/Avaaz via Getty Images

“It is as if a tsunami had hit the city,” said a resident who returned to Sudan’s El Fasher only to find it destroyed and largely abandoned after the Rapid Support Forces’ (RSF) October 2025 takeover.

For 18 months, the RSF laid siege to the city, digging a trench around it in its final months, forcing starving residents to survive on animal fodder, until even that ran out. 

In the siege’s final weeks, the RSF increasingly used drones to target government soldiers, aligned armed groups, and civilians who were ensnared in a tiny area, leaving many maimed or dead. 

During the last days of the offensive, the RSF committed multiple massacres. The UN Human Rights Office estimates over 6,000 people were killed in just three days.

RSF fighters also committed widespread sexual violence against women and girls and ran a massive abduction-for-ransom racket, killing those who couldn’t pay. Thousands of civilians may still languish in RSF dungeons. 

Colombian private military contractors trained in UAE military bases were deployed alongside the RSF on the front lines, operating drones and artillery. Survivors’ accounts confirm the contractors were present during some of the worst atrocities.

The UN fact-finding mission on Sudan concluded that the RSF campaign bore the “hallmarks of genocide,” adding to an increasingly damning body of evidence of the RSF’s serious crimes. 

Governments did not act to prevent these atrocities. In the aftermath, several governments formed a coalition to prevent further atrocities and promote justice in Sudan, and they now need to act together to meet survivors’ demands that those responsible be brought to justice.

One year on, RSF commander Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo “Hemedti” has not even been sanctioned – bar previously by the President Biden administration - despite the crimes of his fighters. The Global Security Services Group (GSSG), the UAE-based company that apparently hired the Colombian fighters, has not faced sanctions, nor is the UAE being held accountable for its military support to the RSF, notwithstanding it violates the UN arms embargo on Darfur. 

At the UN Security Council, the US is pushing for a countrywide extension of the arms embargo. Yet the existing UN arms and sanctions regime has only been renewed for a few months, and the publication of its Panel of Experts’ latest report has been delayed.

Unlike tsunamis, the horrors of El Fasher were preventable. One year on, global actors should ensure it never happens again, take bold steps to protect civilians, and hold those responsible to account.
