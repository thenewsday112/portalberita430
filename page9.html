#388. This 33-Song Playlist Is What Jess McClain Uses to Train for Crushing Marathons

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

For the American course record holder from the 2026 Boston Marathon—who is about to take on the New York City Marathon—music is an integral part of training.

We earn a commission for products purchased through some links in this article. Why Trust Us?

Good music can often make the difference between a rave run and a blown goal. That’s why we’re putting together 90-minute playlists each month chock-full of songs with enough of a beat to keep you moving. For even more tunes, check out our list of The Best Running Playlists to Power Every Pace and Distance.

Jess McClain learned during her first stint as a professional runner that too much of a good thing and singular focus can turn bad. The Arizona native ran competitively in high school, mostly racing 5K and 10K races, and later at Stanford University, where she became a seven-time NCAA Division I All-American. In 2015, however, she went pro, signed with Brooks, and that’s when things took a turn for the worse.

“Through high school and college, running was just so fun; I had other things going on at all times: extracurriculars, stuff with friends on the weekends, football games, and clubs,” she says. “With professional running, I was so excited to go all in, but I realized very quickly I’m not one-dimensional. It’s really hard for me not to have other buckets to pour myself into.”

Back in the 2010s, pros were mostly all about running, and not as much about side hustles and other hobbies. “This is the way it has to be if I want to go all in: I have to live, train, breathe, eat, and sleep running,” McClain says. That all-or-nothing mindset led to repeated injuries and burnout, which forced McClain to part ways with her sponsor in 2018 and, at 26, move back to Phoenix with her parents.

Over the next few years, McClain took a full-time marketing job and got married. “It was a good reset for me,” McClain says. “It gave me a lot of perspective and helped me detach my identity from running. I’d been running competitively since I was 12, so I really didn’t know who I was or what I wanted outside of that. I needed self-discovery time to find out who Jess is when she’s not racing.”

Just when she started considering a return to running, the pandemic shut down racing. But then, in 2022, right before her 30th birthday, McClain raced the Mesa Marathon, her first 26.2 miles, on a lark, training only six weeks before the big day, and finishing in 2:33:34. She qualified for the 2024 Olympic Marathon Trials, where she placed fourth. At the 2024 New York City Marathon and 2025 Boston Marathon, she scooped up top 10 placements. And at the 2026 Boston Marathon, McClain broke the American course record with a time of 2:20:49.

She returned to her sponsor, Brooks. This go-round feels different, she says. “I’ve just had so much fun,” she says. “It took that reset and step back to rediscover why I love racing and getting the best out of myself, but I’m able to do everything else in life to some extent, too. It took a minute to find my recipe, but I did.”

Part of reclaiming her love of running came through music, something that’s been with her since her early days of running. “I remember having the clunky MP3 player and the old headphones that if you moved them a certain way, the music would scratch,” she says, laughing. “Now I am somebody who likes music and podcasts, definitely on my easy runs or solo runs.” Sometimes she’ll put her phone in the back pocket of her sports bra and play music aloud during track workouts for an extra boost.

“I’m a Swiftie for sure—I grew up with her, and we’re the same age, so it’s a nostalgic thing,” she says, “but I love the teenage angst in ‘90s alternative punk rock.” She’s recently been listening to Taking Back Sunday, My Chemical Romance, Matchbox Twenty, and Papa Roach as well.

Runner’s World asked McClain to guest-DJ a running playlist, and she’s put together quite the treat. Her playlist, of course, includes plenty of Taylor Swift, but also some blink-182, Hoodie Allen, and The Veronicas to give it a bit of edge. It may just be the boost you need as well!

This is the gear you actually need to make it to 26.2.

Last year, when I trained for my first marathon, I went a bit gear-crazy. I searched high and low for all the best products to help me get through training and across the finish line. And, I had a lot of fun doing it. I tested out dozens of sneakers, agonized over my race day outfit, and indulged in all the high-tech recovery gadgets (I have a recovery tool for basically every part of my body, from head to toe).

This year, my approach to gear is a lot simpler because I know what I like. More importantly, I know what works. For example: I’m going on two training cycles now of exclusively using Gu Raspberry Lemonade gels as my fuel on long runs. Yeah, when something is good, I’m dedicated.

So, while some of my training staple top spots haven’t budged—like my Bombas running socks, Garmin Forerunner 570, and Therabody JetBoots Pro Plus—that just means the things in this year’s Marathon Awards really impressed me.

I’m still loyal to many of the products in last year’s roundup of my faves, but these are the new additions that deserve a spot on the list.

I have been pretty dead-set on my Nike Vomero Plus (which I wore throughout training and on race day last year) as my max cushioned shoe for everyday runs for a while—but these sneaks from Hoka finally gave them some competition for that spot. They’re less pillowy and more structured, so I feel more in control on a run, and my heel and ankle have more support. During training this time around, I found that my feet started to crave a bit more of that stability from a shoe, so I switched over to the Cliftons to get me through my peak mileage. They hold their own on easy and long runs when it comes to comfort, and feel even better than the Nikes when I’m doing my speed intervals on the track.

There comes a point in training when I simply cannot get myself to wear anything but sneakers. My feet and body just need the support, even at work or running errands or going out with friends. The new Eliot Ryder from Tracksmith is a pillowy soft shoe that cushions every step. Made for running, it could totally double as your easy run go-to, but I’ve been living in it outside of logging miles as well. The shoe’s design also makes it a little more fashionable than other max-cushioned trainers that scream “running shoe.”

Recovery slides are definitely new for my training this year. After dealing with some mysterious foot aches and pains this time around, I started to wear slides around my apartment to avoid walking around flat-footed. Compared to others I tried, these are the softest and offer the most arch support.

I ran my favorite half marathon to date (the Lululemon SeaWheeze half) in this tank—so I’m a little sentimental. But still: it has the classic soft and breathable material I love Lululemon for, it’s super comfortable and lightweight over long distances, and comes in bright colors that will get you spotted on the course. I’m very much considering ironing my name onto the one I have and wearing it on race day.

I trained during the summer months in New York City, so a lightweight top was critical. This one from Salomon kept me comfortable throughout training (and also has a really cool, elevated look). I cannot stress enough just how lightweight, breathable, and quick-drying this fabric is. Truly a gem of a find!

Now that the weather is cooling down, this top from Rhone is my favorite for an early fall day. The material is soft and breathable, making it a perfect transition layer. And, I think the crop and mesh design make it cuter than other running long sleeves.

I would say the biggest thing I didn’t nail down last year was a running vest, so I’m excited to share that I’ve got one that I loved this time around. The big problem with last year’s (unnamed) vest is that the water bottles were always popping out. They stay put in this one from Hoka, which also feels lightweight and easily stores everything I need to bring with me. The closures are easy to work, it feels really secure and comfortable across long distances, and I like the pop the purple gives my usually all-black running outfit.

When all my laundry is clean, these are the first pair of shorts I grab above every other option. The main reason: I want to be comfortable on a run, and these are the most comfortable shorts I have by far. They don’t ride up, provide compression—but not too much compression—and have a softer feel. They have two pockets along the back (one zippered, where I keep my keys) and a mesh one (where I throw my empty gels), plus one on the right thigh (where I keep my phone).

This pair from Bandit also hits all the important notes in running shorts: they don’t ride up, offer compression, and are comfortable even on double-digit mile runs. These shorts also have extra grippy material inside to help them stay put and have pockets on both thighs and two along the back center, which is helpful if you have more things to carry with you on a run. (Come race day, when I don’t plan to wear a vest because I won’t need to carry my own water supply, I’m going to be happy to have an extra pocket to hold my gels!)

Lululemon’s low-impact Flow Y bra got an upgrade to be more supportive for higher-impact activities. The racerback bra has a mesh panel along the back too, making it more breathable on hot days. While training through the summer, I mostly end up running in just a sports bra, and this one also has a fun pattern to show off.

Not to be dramatic, but these hair ties from Goody changed my life. The other weekend, I pulled up Woody Allen’s Manhattan (1979) on YouTube because I’ve been starved for the highbrow and the funny, a rare marriage that movies used to achieve with some regularity, but now hardly ever bother to attempt. 

Let’s disregard the plot, here, and the characters, for there is a great deal about both that resists easy or simple discussion in the present American climate, and focus instead on the surface: the look of the film, the feel of it, the particular New York it conjures and holds up to the light like a jeweler with a loupe.

My feeling for New York City has always come in a peculiar Southern blend of love and affliction. I don’t care for the rudeness, or the flat, clipped music of the accent, the way a sentence up there lands like a screen door.

But some years ago—high school, through college, on into my early twenties—my family made a yearly trip north around Christmas, either before the holiday or just after it. Mother, father, sister, brother, and myself. We took rooms near Times Square. We saw the shows. We ate at the restaurants with the heavy menus and the heavier check.

My mother and sister went shopping. The first year, my father, brother, and I made the mistake of going along, and we stood there in Macy’s—the famous one—waiting among the racks like the accused waiting outside a courtroom, until finally we drifted, defeated, to the section where they sell the beds.

And there, without much discussion, the three of us climbed up onto the display mattresses and took our naps in full view of the shoppers, three Southern males asleep in a snowdrift of goose-down pillows while New Yorkers and tourists alike rushed past us toward their purchases. We never went shopping with the women again.

Manhattan opens with a voice reciting its own first sentence, over and over, dissatisfied, revising itself in real time: “Chapter One. He adored New York City. He idolized it all out of proportion…” What we are watching, before a single frame of story unspools, is the composition of New York, its narration into existence by a man who admits, within the first 30 seconds, that his version of it is a romance rather than a record.

Isaac Davis, played by Allen, drafts Manhattan, discards the draft, drafts it again, each revision a further layer of gloss over a city that by the film’s own admission was never quite there to begin with. Gershwin swells. The skyline assembles itself in black-and-white, denuded of the actual chromatic clutter of a real metropolis: no exhaust-brown, no bodega-awning red, no garbage-day green.

The result is not less than New York, but more: a hyperreal locus, a city more New York than New York, purified into its own advertisement. By the time the narration settles on its final version of the sentence, we have watched a myth being built in front of us, brick by adoring brick, and we have watched it happen with the confidence of a myth that knows it will outlast its subject.

Consider, in this light, why the film insists on monochrome: color would have been the honest choice, but the lesser one. The black-and-white suggests a setting already understood as the past. Allen may have shot it that way because that’s how he remembered the city from boyhood. The image does not become nostalgia, but arrives already nostalgic, already a simulation of an image of a city, twice removed from the referent before the referent has even appeared.

The formal choices reward the kind of patient gazing that asks not what a shot means, but what it does: how it organizes the eye, how it withholds and releases. Willis’s camera holds wide and still. And it is Allen—small, voluble, anxious—who is made to cross the frame, dwarfed by the buildings on either side. The city is never made to accommodate the man. The compositions insist on this dynamic with a kind of moral clarity: architecture as fixed, permanent, indifferent; the human figure as transient, comic, and a little absurd against so much stone. There’s no accident in the fact that the funniest lines are often delivered in the widest shots: the humor is small; the buildings aren’t.

The bench scene deserves a word of correction before anything else, since even devoted admirers of the film misremember it: the silhouette behind Isaac and Mary at dawn is not the Brooklyn Bridge, but the Queensboro—the 59th Street Bridge, the one Simon and Garfunkel made feel groovy—shot from a little park at Sutton Place, the bench itself (according to my Internet searches) trucked in by the crew for that single image and trucked out again after.

Which is, in its own way, the whole method of the film in miniature: even the most iconic, most seemingly eternal image of New York on offer here was a temporary prop set down in front of a bridge for one morning’s shoot. The permanence is manufactured.

The film’s two registers—the melancholy of the story and the euphoria of the imagery—do not cancel each other out so much as run on parallel tracks, and the gap between those tracks is where the picture does its real work. Consider the function of the montage sequences against that of the dialogue scenes. The montages are non-diegetic in feeling even when diegetically grounded: they exist to establish a rhythm of civic wonder, asking nothing of the viewer but admiration.

The dialogue scenes, by contrast, are shot with almost no cutting at all: long, static two-shots, characters talking past each other in real time, the visual monotony forcing attention onto the halting, self-undermining texture of what is actually being said. The film is, formally speaking, telling us two different things simultaneously and daring us to notice the seam: the city as sublime, continuous, orchestral; the people within it as fragmented, hesitant, unable to complete a sentence about their own feelings without doubling back on it, exactly as the opening narration doubled back on itself.

The visual grandeur is never undercut by irony—Allen does not mock the skyline—but it is placed in a kind of formal tension with human smallness that neither element resolves. That unresolved tension, held shot after shot without comment, is the melancholy, and it’s built into the cutting pattern before it is ever spoken aloud.

Now, alas, to the present city—the real one—as it stands to be reorganized, and to the politics of the mayor presently promising to reorganize it.

New York has been bleeding people for years. The arithmetic of it is no longer a matter of dispute: the city and state have shed net domestic migrants every year for over a decade, hundreds of thousands of taxpayers relocating to Florida, Texas, and the Carolinas, taking with them not just households, but the tax base itself, since a disproportionate share of those leaving sit at the top of the income ledger.

Economists have written this story many times over. They generally tell it thus: rent regulation suppressing housing supply, tax rates chasing away the earners who fund the very programs meant to help everyone else, business formation slowing as regulatory and labor costs climb, the tax burden left to redistribute itself across a shrinking and less mobile base.

It is, by now, a familiar and well-documented indictment, and further democratic-socialist experimentation of the kind Zohran Mamdani proposes (price controls, municipal enterprises, steeper taxation of capital) is likely only to accelerate a trend already well underway. What economists rarely pause to mention, in this careful accounting of bad economics, is the bad aesthetics: the fact that a shrinking, flattened, price-controlled city is not only a poorer city, but a less legible one, a city that stops producing the aspirational, ambitious textures that an environment needs to be worth writing a Manhattan about in the first place.

Rent freezes, city-owned grocery stores, free buses, government-run childcare, union-built housing pinned beneath the same frozen rents, higher taxation on the wealthy who have historically financed the city’s theater of distinction: this is a program that wants New York to stop signifying, to stop chasing visual excess, to level unnecessary beauty, and to disappear the glorified aesthetics that defined Manhattan (the film).

The free market is a machine for producing urban variety and diversity. It sorts neighborhoods into strata, forces competition among restaurants and shops and doormen and galleries to distinguish themselves, and out of that sorting comes precisely the texture Isaac Davis fell in love with: the specific bookstore, the specific delicatessen, the specific bench.

Price signals are semiotic: a rent freeze not only caps a number, but also removes the mechanism by which neighborhoods differentiate themselves through investment, turnover, and risk. A city-owned grocery store cannot, by definition, be a particular grocery store as Zabar’s is particular because its entire justification is that it’s the same everywhere, fungible, a public utility rather than a character.

Today’s Manhattan looks different from Allen’s black-and-white mise-en-scène partly because of the regulations governing its buildings: facade inspection mandates have wrapped whole blocks in sidewalk sheds for years at a time, and emissions caps are pushing owners to retrofit prewar towers with electric heat pumps, through-wall units, and rooftop equipment.

Universal childcare and free buses, worthy as they may sound to the ear of compassion, are administered goods, which, besides being economically harmful to the very communities they purport to help, do not accumulate the patina of specificity, tenacity, drive: the worn brass rail, the eccentric owner, the 40 years of small adjustments to changing taste. These details turn a mere service into a grand milieu that a person can photograph in black-and-white and mean something by it. You can’t rent-control your way to a Rizzoli’s.

The specific aesthetic New York that this film so lovingly constructs was the product of an economy that permitted radical unevenness, permitted some blocks to become Sutton Place and others to remain what they were. The old New York could be ugly in reality and beautiful in art because it left room for artists to look upward, to take its disorder and inequality and noise and transfigure them into myth. The danger of Mamdani’s New York is not merely that it becomes poorer or more regulated, but that it becomes aesthetically downward-looking, a city preoccupied with administering what is wrong with it rather than generating the beauty that makes people want to imagine it as something more.

A politics organized entirely around flattening unevenness in the name of universal provision will tend to produce a city whose surfaces mean less. Mamdani’s New York, should it fully arrive, promises a setting that is more equal and more affordable and, quite possibly, less quotable, a city no future Woody Allen would find himself compelled to open his picture with “Chapter one. Which brings me back to that Christmas Macy’s, my father asleep on a showroom mattress, three Southerners playing hooky from the shopping. I think that’s what I’m partially mourning when I mourn the New York of Manhattan: not the wealth, not even the skyline, but the specificity, the sense that the city was a collection of unique rooms rather than a portfolio of interchangeable services.

The people I know who have left for Texas, for Florida, for the redder counties of this reddening country, will tell you they left the taxes, the crime, the cost. But listen long enough, and what they are really grieving, underneath the ledger, is that the New York which could still surprise you is going, has been going for a while now, and no rent freeze, however kindly meant, will bring it back, because you cannot legislate a city into being irreplaceable. You can only leave it alone long enough, and love it hard enough, to let it become so on its own, the way, once, at dawn, on a borrowed bench under a borrowed bridge, it did.

#143. Brighton injury news: Double boost confirmed by Fabian Hurzeler before Man City EFL Cup tie

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

By Oliver Thomas | 27 Oct 2026 14:04

Brighton & Hove Albion head coach Fabian Hurzeler has provided a fresh update on the fitness of his squad ahead of Wednesday’s EFL Cup fourth-round tie away against Manchester City.

The Seagulls are seeking to avoid a third successive game without a win or goal, after following up a goalless draw with Jablonec in the Conference League with a 2-0 Premier League defeat at Liverpool on Sunday.

Full-back Ferdi Kadioglu and midfield summer signing Chema Andres both picked up hip injuries in the loss at Anfield and Hurzeler has confirmed that both players will need to be assessed ahead of the trip to the Etihad Stadium.

“It's just a hit on the hips,” Hurzeler said at a press conference on Tuesday. “Of course, it's very painful, so it's something where both players have to deal with the pain.

“Pain for every player feels differently, so we have to see how they train today, if it's possible, if they can train.

“If yes, then of course there will be an option for tomorrow, but they definitely should be both available for the weekend.”

Kaoru Mitoma, Femi Azeez, Pascal Struijk (all thigh), Evan Ferguson (ankle) and Stefanos Tzimas (knee) are five Brighton players who remain sidelined with injuries.

However, Hurzeler has revealed that Mats Wieffer and Yankuba Minteh are in contention to feature in some capacity against Man City after recovering from long-term injuries.

Versatile midfielder/full-back Wieffer has been sidelined since August with a knee injury, while winger Minteh is yet to play for the Seagulls this term since sustaining a serious leg injury in pre-season.

“That's a positive one, that Minteh and Wieffer will be back. Having new faces back in the group always brings new energy,” said Hurzeler.

"The injured players who were out for a long time always have a point to prove. They want to get back in the team.

“You feel it from day one when they come on the pitch that they want to show they belong in the team, and that's always helpful to have this competition for places, it lifts the energy in the training sessions.

“We are all looking forward to having these players back because, before the injuries, they were big players for us. Of course, the team is playing very well, so it also means that the returning players have to raise their standards, but that's only positive for our culture.”

Brighton are preparing for back-to-back away games against Man City in the space of four days, with their EFL Cup tie followed by a Premier League battle with the table-toppers on Saturday.

“We experienced it a few years ago when we played twice against Chelsea,” said Hurzeler.  “It's about competing tomorrow then recovering, adjusting, adapting things, and competing again - it's really going from game to game.

“We don't focus on Saturday. We want to be the best-prepared team for Wednesday, and that's the main focus. We don't try to be distracted by the weekend game. We play a do-or-die game, so we are fully focused on this.”

Brighton have already prevailed on Manchester soil this season, coming from two goals down at half time to best Man United 3-2 in the EFL Cup third round last month.

However, the Seagulls have failed to win any of their previous 17 visits to Man City across all competitions (D3 L14), albeit drawing both of their last two in the Premier League.
