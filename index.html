#417. Four Ways to Help Newcomers Feel Like They Belong

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Become a subscribing member today. Help us continue to bring “the science of a meaningful life” to you and to millions around the globe.

Greater Good Science Center • Magazine • In Action • In Education

Liz Cahill was born and raised in Philadelphia. Her whole family is from Philadelphia, going back generations. One day, she was offered an internship in the Bay Area and decided to make the leap, despite not knowing anything about it or anyone there.

“I said goodbye to everything I knew and loved in Philadelphia and came to the Bay. Not knowing a soul.”

When she arrived, it hit her. “I was like, wow, I’m in a place that like, literally nobody knows me. So if I am dead in a ditch, like nobody will figure it out for a long time.”

This could have been a story of loneliness and disconnection, but it wasn’t, because someone took her under their wings—actually two strangers, her roommates Lauren and Deirdre. “They took me in and welcomed me and brought me into their whole social world, which showed me so much love.”

This isn’t the experience for most people. Darilys Matos Acevedo grew up in Puerto Rico. “In Puerto Rico, a stranger talks to you all the time. If you went down the street and you talked to somebody, if you made eye contact . . . you had to smile at them or you said good morning or good afternoon.”

Then she moved to Boston for graduate school. “I had never even visited the place. I knew nobody here.” And where she got smiles and small talk in Puerto Rico, in Boston she “got glares.” “And I’m like, oh, OK. You don’t do any of that here.” It was painful and created a large void in her life.

As Darilys talked to other Latinos in Boston, she assumed “I’m the only one who feels this way, but I wasn’t. There was like a whole group of people that felt like that.”

Their stories are different, but they reveal the same truth.

Moving doesn’t simply change where we live. It interrupts the invisible web of relationships that makes everyday life feel familiar, that makes us feel like we belong. And the majority of the 13 million American adults who move each year experience this.

At the US Chamber of Connection, our recent study on social connection in America explored the impact of life transitions on social connection. It found that 40% of people navigating a relocation struggle with social connection, and they report 35% more barriers to connection compared to everyone else. Two of the most common barriers are not knowing where to start and not wanting to go out alone.

The impact is lasting, as someone shared with us. “Two years ago, I was going on a vacation, and I could not find ONE neighbor to water my plants while I was away. I had lived in this building on and off for over 15 years. I was mortified. How could it be that I had nobody in my building to help with such a small task?”

Moving ranks among the most stressful life events a person can experience, on par with divorce and job loss. But the stress isn’t just logistical; it’s psychological. Moving severs the informal ties and daily routines that quietly anchor our sense of who we are and where we belong. The result is a predictable but rarely named experience: not just loneliness, but identity dislocation. The question isn’t only “who do I know here?” but “who am I here?”

This month, we're teaming up with the US Chamber of Connection for Welcome Week. Together, we're mobilizing communities, neighborhoods, and organizations nationwide to do one simple thing this September: open the door to a new friend. Make a pledge to invite someone in and help us reach our goal to make 100,000 people feel welcomed!

To understand the scale of that loss, consider what a typical adult social network requires to build: roughly 200 hours to develop a close friend, 90 hours for a friend, and five hours of repeated interaction for each of the weak ties (neighbors, familiar faces, community contacts) that make daily life feel anchored. A typical adult carries around 1,300 hours of accumulated relationship time. When you move, you leave all of it behind and start from zero.

This is also, paradoxically, what makes the first year after a move one of the most powerful windows for connection. Disruption breaks old habits and makes people unusually open to forming new ones, a phenomenon researchers call the habit discontinuity effect. People who have just moved are more likely to join things, try things, and reach out than they were six months earlier at home.

What closes that window isn’t lack of desire. Research by Nicholas Epley at the University of Chicago finds that people systematically underestimate how much strangers want to connect and overestimate how awkward the attempt will be. Both the newcomer and the potential neighbor hold back, each assuming the other isn’t interested. Both tend to be wrong.

And the effects of getting it right last longer than most people expect. Vanessa Elias, the founder of Block Party USA, shared with us the story of a neighbor who brought her family a pie when she moved in as a six year old. She remembers her name, Mrs. Fisher, decades later.

What would happen if welcoming became a civic practice instead of an individual virtue? This year, the US Chamber of Connection recruited over 250 people across 25 states to run creative experiments that aimed to help others feel more welcome in their neighborhoods. They were trained on the science of connection and worked together across the country to support each other. We call them the Welcome Committee.

For many of the volunteers, they signed up because they had an experience like Liz. One shared that “when we arrived to our new town we felt welcomed, surprised, and alive through these touches, and wanted to build more.” Or as another volunteer explained, “people are still welcoming me and I still feel at home here. So it gave me this strong sense of duty to pay that forward. I wanna create spaces where people can land and feel comfortable.”

For others, it came from an experience more akin to Darilys’s. “I would have loved to find this kind of thing when I moved,” shared one early volunteer. “I’ve been in my neighborhood and city since 2019 and have yet to make close friends in the neighborhood or city where I live. It feels scary and uncomfortable to get out there. But I’m realizing the huge impact of not having close friends. I’m ready to make the effort. Because if I’m feeling this way, I can’t be the only one.”

They each began by inviting a neighbor for a conversation. They shared their stories of their neighborhoods growing up. Dreamed about the ideal neighborhood together. Explored the Six Points of Connection, a clear and actionable checklist for individuals and leaders to define and strengthen social bonds. And then they explored how they might together make the neighborhood more connected.

They ran some creative experiments. Lindsay turned their suburban garage door into a community message board. Kaitlin created a traveling tin of homemade baked goods that moves neighbor to neighbor down the street, each recipient required to bake something and pass it on to someone new. Sarah hosted a gathering for other people named Sara(h) in her town. Courtney started flyering the neighborhood to organize a dog gathering in someone’s backyard. Nithya left a flower and a handwritten note for each of the five nearest neighbors inviting them to coffee.

Many of the volunteers nostalgically tried things they remembered from their childhood or had seen in a movie. Kate moved an iron chair and table to her front step and started having her morning coffee there facing the street. Jon made lemonade with his kids and set out donuts for anyone walking by. Chet started introducing himself on his daily dog walks. Anne organized a multi-household yard sale across eight houses. And many of them hosted potlucks and block parties.

They all expressed knowing “how meaningful it can be to walk into a new space and find someone who is genuinely glad you are there.” And they wanted to make people feel that way.

As the volunteers conducted their experiments, they began to share their findings. They mirrored much of what we have learned from social scientists over the last 50 years. Four themes emerged that help to inform how we can all do more to help new residents, and any neighbor, feel more welcomed.

1. The first step is the most important. Stop playing “invite chicken.” Many of our volunteers shared a version of the same experience: sitting at home hoping someone would call and invite them to hang out, attend an event, or even just watch Netflix together. They assumed that the people they wanted to call were probably busy or uninterested. Those people were likely sitting at home thinking the same thing. Nobody called. Everyone stayed alone.

Call it invite chicken: a standoff where everyone loses despite everyone wanting the same thing. The barrier isn’t reality; it’s anticipation. Someone just has to go first.

And sometimes, when we do break the game of chicken, we don’t get the response we wanted. When Hazel hosted her first neighborhood coffee gathering, one neighbor texted that he was too nervous to come. She didn’t give up. She texted back encouraging him. He came. At one of Elizabeth’s gatherings in San Diego, a man had been dragged along by his wife, who promised they’d stay just five minutes. He was the last to leave.

We are poor forecasters of our own social experiences. People overweight the discomfort of initiation and underweight the pleasure of connection. The host who sends the follow-up text, the wife who insists on five minutes, isn’t being pushy. They’re correcting for a cognitive error the reluctant person can’t see from the inside.

2. We are more motivated working together. Partner with someone. Erin hosted a neighborhood gathering in New York City and reflected afterward on what she’d do differently. Her top lesson: “I appreciated the help I did get from neighbors and wished I’d had a co-planner to share more of the work with.”

This is consistent with what research finds: People are significantly more likely to initiate and sustain kind and helpful behavior when they’re doing it alongside someone else. A partner doesn’t just make the work easier; they make follow-through more likely, because the social cost of backing out now involves someone else. Several Welcome Committee volunteers named the US Chamber of Connection cohort itself as the thing that got them out the door. Not the training, not the tools, but the fact that others were doing it, too.

3. We gain comfort with repeat exposure. Build a rhythm. Hansen has been hosting a monthly porch gathering (coffee, bagels, same spot) for two years. Recently, he watched neighbors spontaneously make plans to go cycling together and quietly organize to look out for a boy on the street whose father is dying of cancer. Neither of those things was planned. They happened because the same people had been attending his events and they had built comfort with each other. Hansen didn’t suggest they help the boy but had created the environment for it to be possible.

Research finds that repeated contact with the same faces makes us like and trust them more, independent of what’s said. Those repeated encounters build weak ties, which are how people find help in a crisis, how trust spreads beyond close friends, how a community starts to look out for itself. The first gathering gets you in the door. Showing up again is what builds the neighborhood.

What these moments share is spontaneity and clarity of intent. Research finds that unexpected gestures create a positive disruption that formal invitations don’t. They bypass the social calculation people run when a stranger approaches, and they signal warmth without asking for anything back. Research finds that being noticed is the precondition for feeling like you belong.

And having a clear role removes the ambiguity that makes first moves feel risky. When Annie said “I’m a Welcome Committee member,” her motives were transparent before she said another word. You don’t need a plan. You need to look up.

Giving to others produces more sustained well-being than spending on yourself. The helper’s high is real. Welcoming a newcomer isn’t just a form of service; it’s one of the highest-return social investments you can make.

Liz knows this firsthand. The woman who arrived in the Bay Area not knowing a soul, wondering if anyone would notice if she disappeared, is now the person who creates spaces for others to land. “People here took a chance on me,” she says. “So it gave me this strong sense of duty to pay that forward. I want to create spaces where people can land and feel comfortable.”

All friendships begin the same way: by talking to strangers. Someone just has to go first. Years later, Liz became the person who notices.

#415. The Overlooked Ways Love Shows Up in Urban Spaces

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Become a subscribing member today. Help us continue to bring “the science of a meaningful life” to you and to millions around the globe.

Greater Good Science Center • Magazine • In Action • In Education

Over the past two decades, my doctoral students and I have focused on studying love, care, kindness, and compassion in urban settings. Why? Because social science research on life in America’s cities is desperately incomplete. Much like the vision of urban life that permeates most of American popular culture, social science research imagines urban areas as sites of overwhelmedness, disengagement, social and moral crisis, threat, and violence.

But, as someone who has lived and worked in large urban centers—New York City, Chicago, Detroit, Istanbul—I know firsthand that urban centers are also sites of human virtue. People in urban contexts, including those living in high-stress, oversurveilled, underresourced communities, draw daily from deep wells of love, compassion, empathy, kindness, and generosity in the service of caring for each other. From holding doors open for one another, to offering seats on buses and trains, to stopping to help with directions, to paying unexpected compliments, city-dwellers create environments that are saturated with powerful but easily missed acts of care.

My former students and I have been eager to understand how love, generosity, and care manifest and operate in the hyperdiverse, densely populated, loud, fast-paced complexities of urban spaces. Through surveys and interviews, we have sought to understand how adults residing in big cities define love, how they see love being expressed, and their views of the cascading effects of giving and receiving care, generosity, and love.

We have learned that the decision to express love and care for others in large cities is not simply a moral or emotional decision. We offer two examples of the lens needed to notice the presence of love in cities.

First, we have learned that identities matter in experiences of compassion, love, and care between strangers. Urban dwellers’ identity (for example, race or gender), lived experience, context, familial, cultural, and legal systems’ realities work together to shape decisions about whether to extend acts of love to people you do not know. For example, Kehinde, a 26-year-old African American man living in a large, densely populated city, described his experience of giving and receiving kindness, care, and love in his city as shaped by how he navigates other people seeing him as a Black man.

“I will see folks all the time that may need a hand with their bags or crossing the street or just little things like that. And I take a second, hesitate,” Kehinde says. “I don’t want to scare them. I don’t want to come off as a threat.”

To get through these moments, Kehinde describes engaging in a practice of analysis and shape-shifting. Before he steps in to offer help, he assesses the environment and changes the way he carries himself so that people will perceive him as a possible helper rather than as a threat. He says: “I smile a lot more. My voice might change a bit.” He might shrink his body and keep his head down, to avoid seeming intimidating. After helping someone, he moves along without lingering. Reflecting, he acknowledges not being his full, true self in these interactions. “I have to realize I’ve been socialized—made to believe that this is what I need to do in order to navigate the situation.” Kehinde’s story highlights the point that identities matter in economies of care. The images and stereotypes that we construct about people come at a cost even in moments of compassion. Stereotypes affect who we perceive as deserving of care, concern, and protection. Stereotypes also inform our expectations about who can be trusted to help. Stereotyping is not unique to urban settings. But because urban settings tend to be especially diverse spaces, they create abundant opportunities to study how identities and the social scripts we hold affect the way that strangers care for each other across lines of difference. What Kehinde and others bring to the fore is the reality that the targets of stereotyping are aware of the ways that they are perceived. They learn that to be seen as helpful rather than as threatening, they need to read situations correctly, make adjustments to their approaches (e.g., keep “appropriate” distance), and exit situations strategically. Targets of stereotyping must strike a balance between their own deep-seated motivations to behave caringly and their desire to stay safe. For individuals like Kehinde, miscalculations can be costly. If, in the effort to behave lovingly and compassionately, someone is misidentified as a threat, that can lead to a range of consequences from being rebuffed and embarrassed to becoming the target of violence, arrest, or death. The end result is that people consciously or unconsciously learn the rules of engagement around helping strangers.

This piece is part of a three-year GGSC project to explore love's role in promoting social well-being, bridging divides, and contributing to a good, meaningful life.

A second lesson that has emerged from studying love in urban settings is that love lives in the built environment of cities. I highlight this lesson in my Positive Psychology in Urban Settings classes. Cities are often saturated with murals, billboards, and symbols that convey messages of love, solidarity, and belonging. As residents move through neighborhood streets, they may encounter signs prominently posted in shop windows or near the entrances of religious institutions that remind them that “Black Lives Matter,” “Love Is Love,” or “Immigrants Are Welcome Here.” People display rainbow flags and national flags from apartment windows, on cars, and in other spaces, declaring both their own identities and pride for and solidarity with those identities. The architecture of urban spaces may also play a role in expressions of love and care. In many urban neighborhoods, porches, stoops, and front steps serve as places of connection and care. Simple greetings (e.g., “Morning, baby!,” “You doing good?”) voiced by neighbors sitting on their steps serve as ways of spatializing love, care, and connection in urban landscapes.

I return here to my initial point. Our studies of urban areas are desperately incomplete and will remain incomplete until we more fully examine how various forms of human goodness—including love and care—flourish in urban settings. Likewise, our understandings of love and care will remain underdeveloped until we explore how these experiences emerge in the most densely populated, diverse, complex social structures in which people live. We will truly understand love when we understand how it is titrated through place, context, history, culture, religion, structures of power, identities, and a host of individual and relational factors. Urban centers give us powerful opportunities to know love.

Jacqueline Mattis, Ph.D., is dean of the School of Arts and Sciences at Rutgers University-Newark. She studies human goodness in urban contexts.

#412. Can Community Programs Reduce Gun Violence?

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Greater Good Science Center • Magazine • In Action • In Education

A new study by researchers at UC Berkeley’s Center for Global Healthy Cities published in the Journal of the American Medical Association Network Open found that the California city of Richmond’s Office of Neighborhood Safety (ONS) was responsible for a 61% reduction in that city’s firearm homicides and injury shootings over the last 14 years.

This is one of the only studies in the country to document the sustained impacts of a community violence intervention program on city-wide firearm violence over more than 10 years.

According to lead author Alice Bruno, a doctoral student in epidemiology at UC Berkeley’s School of Public Health, “we leveraged data from dozens of comparable cities across the United States to estimate what firearm violence would have looked like in Richmond without the ONS, Peacemaker Fellowship. We found that the Fellowship contributed to long-term reductions in violence, and that the declines only grew as the program operated.” Other UC Berkeley coauthors include Jason Corburn; Mahasin Mujahid, chair of the Division of Epidemiology; Sean Darling-Hammond; and Juan Cabrera.

Sam Vaughn, director of ONS, said, “The statistics back up what we have seen in the streets: fewer shootings, fewer deaths, and a healthier community overall.”

Homicide rates are down across the country; the FBI recently released data showing murder rates are down a record 18.1% nationally. However, Richmond has consistently outperformed national declines for over a decade, suggesting the results are due to that city’s innovative, street-outreach interventions and their mentorship of active firearm offenders, a program called Operation Peacemaker Fellowship, which was instituted in 2010. The Fellowship uses a public health approach to preventing gun violence.

According to James Houston, the program manager of the ONS who oversees the day-to-day implementation of the Peacemaker Fellowship, including managing 14 street-outreach workers who currently serve 123 participants, “the credit goes to the young people making better decisions and the team that gives them support, mentorship, and encouragement every day to make a change.”

According to Shasa Curl, Richmond city manager, Richmond had over 300 police officer full-time equivalent in 2014 and 180 in 2020, but was down to 121 active sworn officers by 2024. Yet during this time, the ONS, through continued city support and grants, was able to expand its staff and increase the number of fellows and participants. Even during the COVID-19 period, the study shows Richmond did not have as significant a spike in firearm violence as most cities did across the Bay Area and nationally.

According to the ONS, the health impacts are lives saved, safer streets, and community members with a renewed positive life outlook.

One former Peacemaker Fellowship participant said, “Before the ONS came to me, I didn’t care if I survived past 19, as long as I had a name in the streets. After my experience with the ONS, with my mentor coming at me every day, I got support for my stress, got a job, paying my bills, and have a kid now. I’m no longer terrorizing my community.”

The paper notes the cascading health benefits for entire communities when gun violence reductions are sustained, such as the case in Richmond. Further research will explore how unique aspects of the ONS might be contributing to declines in gun violence, including that the program is a city government agency, independent from the police department, has had consistent funding, has trained and retained many of the same outreach workers for over a decade (these city staff are well paid with benefits), and delivers individualized supports to each participant based on their needs, such as food and housing assistance, cognitive behavioral therapy, addiction and anger management counseling, and other social services aimed at both reducing gun violence risk and addressing trauma.

#318. Michael Strahan Shares the Words That Helped His Daughter Through Brain Cancer Treatment

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

  • Michael Strahan opened up to Healthline about his daughter Isabella’s brain cancer diagnosis and how the experience strengthened their relationship.
  • After undergoing surgery, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy for medulloblastoma, Isabella has returned to college and is embracing life after treatment.
  • Strahan shared how he helped his daughter maintain hope during treatment and why they’re speaking out to support other families affected by cancer.

For years, Michael Strahan appeared in commercials for St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital, delivering a message he never imagined would become so personal: “No parent should have to hear that their child has cancer.”

But in 2023, the beloved TV host and former NFL player faced that reality when his daughter, Isabella, was diagnosed with brain cancer at 19 years old.

“[That] line that I used to deliver, usually at the end of the commercial…here I am actually filling that line in my life in real time,” Strahan told Healthline.

Strahan believes his longtime connection with St. Jude was more than a coincidence. After years of advocating for families affected by childhood cancer, he found himself on the other side of that experience.

“When I needed that family…they were there for me,” he said. “I’m just appreciative, and I think things come back to you; give out good, good comes back.”

Facing brain cancer at 19 years old

In October 2023, Isabella began experiencing vomiting, headaches, and balance issues during her freshman year at the University of Southern California (USC). Tests revealed that she had medulloblastoma, a fast-growing cancerous brain tumor located in the cerebellum. She underwent emergency brain surgery, radiation therapy, and four rounds of chemotherapy.

While medulloblastoma typically occurs in children between the ages of 3 and 9, it can also affect young adults like Isabella.

“[The] biology in adolescents and young adults can differ meaningfully from pediatric cases, requiring specialized expertise at centers of excellence for this disease,” Arnab Chakravarti, MD, director of the Brain Tumor Program at The Ohio State University, told Healthline.

Medulloblastoma is sometimes confused with glioblastoma, the most common type of malignant primary brain tumor in adults. Glioblastoma is particularly aggressive, with a median overall survival of just 15 months and a 5-year survival rate of less than 10%.

However, Chakravarti explained that medulloblastoma has a significantly higher cure rate than glioblastoma.

“[This is] a point of genuine hope that deserves to be communicated clearly,” he said.

Radiation therapy is a cornerstone of medulloblastoma treatment. Isabella received proton therapy, which uses precisely targeted radiation to treat tumors while limiting exposure to surrounding healthy tissue.

“For medulloblastoma, this physical precision is transformative,” Chakravarti said. “Proton craniospinal irradiation dramatically reduces [collateral] exposure — preserving cochlear function and hearing, reducing cardiovascular risk, protecting the thyroid, and most critically, sparing developing brain tissue in ways that translate into measurably better neurocognitive outcomes years after treatment.”

He added that multiple prospective studies have shown that children and young adults treated with proton craniospinal irradiation experience less cognitive decline and better quality of life compared to those treated with conventional radiation.

Today, Isabella is back at USC, embracing the everyday routines she once had to put on hold.

“I am back at USC, so…it’s been great, in the midst of exams, so it’s a little stressful, but I actually [am] inviting the stress in because at one point I didn’t have exams, so I’m happy to be back,” Isabella told Healthline.

Along with attending classes, Isabella is enjoying activities such as working out and cooking. She continues to undergo MRI scans of her head and spine every three months, but said that after another year, the scans will become less frequent, occurring every six months.

“But really…I don’t have a lot of doctor’s appointments,” she said.

Her outlook on life has also changed.

“It feels like a brand-new life, but it’s a good one. And that’s wonderful,” Isabella said.

Cancer strengthened their father-daughter bond

Isabella said her family’s support played an important role in helping her through cancer treatment. Spending so much time together in the hospital brought them closer as they watched movies and played cards and board games to pass the time.

“[It] really, really bonds you to people, and just having my dad and my whole family there was something that [you] can’t beat,” she told Healthline.

Michael said that despite the difficult circumstances, he cherishes the time he spent with his daughter. Before her diagnosis, Isabella was a typical teenager who spent much of her time at school or with friends.

“They say you spend the most time with your kid for the first few years of their life, and then once school starts, you don’t get a chance to spend as much time as you would like with them,” he said.

While Michael said he and Isabella already had a close relationship, her diagnosis brought the family even closer and helped them reflect on what mattered most.

“[What] was really important was family, and relationships, and the love that you have for each other, and the support you have to give each other,” he said. “I think that our relationship now is better than ever.”

Today, Michael makes an effort to visit Isabella at USC whenever he’s in Los Angeles during the NFL season.

“Some weekends she [has time for me]. Some she doesn’t,” he said. “But just to have that relationship with your child, where they want to hang out with you, is a good feeling, and that’s the relationship that we have.”

Turning experience into advocacy

Michael and Isabella are sharing their story to raise awareness about brain cancer and offer hope to other families facing similar challenges.

They recently participated in a fireside chat in New York City hosted by Real Chemistry and SMAC, where they discussed their family’s experience navigating Isabella’s diagnosis, mental health, caregiving, and the impact of sharing their story.

Throughout her treatment, Isabella also documented her experience through a series of YouTube vlogs and the ABC primetime documentary, “Life Interrupted: Isabella Strahan’s Fight to Beat Cancer.”

For Michael, helping others is a value he learned from his parents. He recalled how his father used his transportation business in Germany to support people in need after retiring from the military.

“[He] would take donations, and use his money and his trucks, and drive stuff to Yugoslavia and Czechoslovakia, and drop it off there at local churches and the shelters for people, who he had no connection with,” said Michael. “So for me, I think I do what I feel is on my heart and what’s important to me.”

Why Michael Strahan believes hope matters when facing cancer

For parents caring for children with cancer, Michael emphasized that it’s OK to feel overwhelmed or emotional. He encouraged them to show their children love and support while helping them maintain hope for the future.

“I think positivity is the main thing because it was so tough on her at the time when she was [saying], ‘I can’t do it. I can’t do this. I can’t do that,’” said Michael.

Michael said he made a conscious effort to replace words like “can’t” and “if” with “when” while encouraging Isabella during treatment.

“Anytime she got down on herself, I’d say, ‘when you go back to college, when you do this, when you do that,’” said Michael. “And I just think the positive attitude and the love that you can give and the confidence that you can display towards your loved one [or] your child who’s going through something like this is the only thing you can do because you’re at the mercy of medicine.”

Now in her senior year at USC, Isabella is looking ahead to life after graduation.

“[There’s] a lot of interesting opportunities and things that I want to pursue,” she said.

Michael has no doubt she will find her way.

#25. Manchester City Premier League charges: What do they mean?

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Since being bought by the UAE in 2008, Manchester City has become one of the world's most successful football clubs. A raft of serious financial charges threatens to undermine this and bring chaos to the Premier League.

Manchester City has won the English Premier League eight times over the last 14 years. Whether those titles will remain is one of a plethora of issues at stake after England's top division charged one of its most high-profile clubs with more than 100 breaches of its financial regulations.

On Tuesday, the Premier League announced that an independent commission had found City had drawn up "sham contracts" as well as "relying on sham agreements to artificially inflate the club's revenues and reduce its costs." These contracts, dubious sponsorships and a number of other methods were, according to the commission's report, used to inflate the club's revenue to the tune of about £900 million (€1.05 billion) over the period between the 2009-10 and 2017-18 seasons.

The case has been ongoing for eight years after initial revelations from Der Spiegel, a German magazine, and Rui Pinto of the 'Football Leaks' website. City continues to deny any wrongdoing.

"The Club is innocent of the accusations made by the Premier League and a comprehensive body of irrefutable evidence exists in support of all of its positions, relating to this case," read a club statement. "Manchester City FC will now pursue the appeal avenues open to it, on the basis that the opinion contains clear material errors, of law, principle and fact, and is unsafe."

On Friday, the club issued a further statement saying it had lodged a "comprehensive Appeal."

The 750-page report was damning, accusing City of inflating contracts and sponsorship deals, running secondary contracts off the books, making "concerted efforts to stop and frustrate the Premier League investigation" and signing off inflated image rights deals to artificially boost their earnings. The report says all this enabled them to pass both Premier League and UEFA financial spending limits.

The Premier League's chief ­executive, Richard Masters, described the case "as the most significant in Premier League history," adding that the club "systematically broke Premier League Rules for nearly a decade."

Although the Premier League, the world's most commercially successful football league, has drawn some praise for its actions, others point out that it took Pinto leaking emails and media reports to take action. The report shows that Manchester City, which had won the league once in 71 years before being taken over by the United Arab Emirates (UAE) in 2008, were consistently posting revenues way higher than any of their more established and successful rivals.

In this context, its the owners. The Abu Dhabi United Group, essentially an investment vehicle for the UAE royal family, bought the club in 2008. Mansour bin Zayed Al Nahyan, know simply as Sheikh Mansour, fronted the takeover. He is a member of the ruling Al Nahyan family and the UAE's vice president and deputy prime minister. Khaldoon Al Mubarak, who also holds several government positions in the UAE, was brought in as chairman.

Nation-state ownership of this type is controversial in European football. Manchester City's owners have long been criticized for the country's human rights record and using the club for sportswashing. Similar criticisms have been made of European champions Paris Saint-Germain, which Qatar owned and City's Premier League rivals Newcastle United, which is owned 85% by Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund.

City's CEO, Ferran Soriano, joined in 2012 as City expanded their ownership into foreign leagues, buying up clubs in the US, Japan and Australia. He replaced former head coach Roberto Mancini with fellow Spaniard Manuel Pellegrini before former Bayern Munich and Barcelona coach Pep Guardiola took over in 2016, ushering in the club's most successful period.

The club immediately started splashing out on big-money transfers after the UAE investment arrived. At the time, neither UEFA nor the Premier League had the tight financial rules they now have. UEFA introduced its Financial Fair Play (FFP) rules for the 2011-12 season, while the Premier League followed with its Profit and Sustainability Rules (PSR) in 2013-14.

City's free spending and relatively low commercial income meant they were likely to fall foul of such rules. The club was handed a conditional €60 million UEFA fine for losses in the 2011-12 and 2012-13 seasons. They would later be banned from the Champions League on similar grounds in 2020 before being reinstated on appeal.

In an attempt to try and comply with these financial rules, City set up a system they called "Project Longbow" in 2012. The commission said much of this was genuine, but one arm of it was an agreement with a third party called Fordham that was, according to the ruling, "little more than a front." Essentially, it reads, the club's UAE owners "would be used (and were used) to enable Fordham to acquire from the club (at a sizeable, artificially inflated price) the club's entitlement to benefit financially from its players' image rights."

In other words, City's owners were pumping money into their own club through vastly inflated sponsorship and image rights deals so their income looked higher and they could spend more, particularly on player transfers and wages. The report also claims that City paid three individuals, whose names are redacted, through a third party to keep them off the books.

That's the question on everyone's lips. So far, no sanctions have been imposed on City, and the appeal will likely drag proceedings out even longer. Meanwhile, the club, which is top of the Premier League, faces Liverpool on October 11, after the international break.

While there is no precedent for such a tranche of charges, clubs have previously fallen foul of the Premier League's PSR regulations. Everton were handed a 10-point penalty for a £19.5 million breach over a period ending with the 2021-22 season. This was based on a Premier League tariff of six points for the initial breach, plus an additional point for every £5 million the club overspent. Given that City's overspending is close to a billion pounds, applying that would certainly relegate them, though the Premier League does not operate England's second-tier league, the Championship.

For now, it looks like City and the league will play on under a cloud. But if the verdict remains after appeal, clubs and individuals whose careers and honors stand to be impacted will likely come forward and litigate. City themselves could be relegated, docked points over successive seasons, face transfer bans or any combination of those things. The titles won in the relevant period could also be stripped.

#19. Manchester City, the UAE and the UK's OM in a political tangle

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Nearly 20 years after arriving in England, Manchester City's UAE owners could exit amid cheating charges. That would pose a challenge for a city — and prime minister — closely tied to Abu Dhabi.

Defiance was the word that stood out as Manchester City returned home for the first time since they were found guilty of 114 financial charges by the Premier League in a case that's made headlines around the world.

City showed a measure of it on the pitch, even as they lost 4-2 to holders, Qatari-owned Paris Saint-Germain, in the Champions League group stages on Wednesday.

Among the home fanbase at the Etihad Stadium, DW found universal support for City's Abu Dhabi owners — headed by Sheikh Mansour, who is also UAE's vice president and deputy prime minister.

"I'm all in with the ownership now," said local fan Matt. "If we end up back in League Two [fourth tier], so be it. We've got 15 years of memories we wouldn't have otherwise and to me it's just an accounting line on a spreadsheet really."

The stadium name itself is a reminder of the takeover that changed this club, and its part of Manchester, in 2008. The national airline of the UAE, Etihad's inflated sponsorship of City is among the key planks of the case against them, while many buildings and businesses around the stadium bear the brand. It's a stadium that's also seen plenty of success of late.

"We've seen things other fans will never see," added fellow City fan Josh. "I think people have got to consider how many people this club has given great memories to, how many people's lives have been changed by it, myself included....you've got to be grateful for that."

Though many in sky blue shared those sentiments, most outside the club now view the trophies won in the relevant period as tarnished. On top of that, critics point to City's reticence in providing evidence in their case, the long-documented human rights abuses in UAE and the often dismissive tone with which the owners have dealt with being found guilty of charges they have now appealed.

But those City fans appear to have one powerful ally in British Prime Minister Andy Burnham. Though he has since sought to tone down his position on Mansour and co. somewhat, the former mayor of Greater Manchester said he "would be really concerned to lose them," after the verdict was announced in late September. "They've been such a huge partner in the building of modern Manchester," he added.

While Burnham has since denied being close to City's ownership, even as City were playing on Wednesday, The Guardian reported a club representative was invited on a government-backed trade delegation to India this week by Burnham's successor as Manchester mayor, Bev Craig.

Earlier the same day, Burnham was forced to confirm he had two additional dinners with the club chair, Khaldoon Al Mubarak, at the Etihad Stadium, having previously declared those only as matchday hospitality. It has become a serious domestic issue for the man who replaced Keir Starmer in July.

What is clear is that Burnham welcomed Abu Dhabi investment during his time in charge of the northwestern city. This came mainly in the form of residential and commercial buildings as well as libraries and sports centers, all popping up in previously run-down areas in and around the stadium.

Georgina Blakeley, a Professor at the University of Huddersfield who wrote a book called "The regeneration of east Manchester," told DW that UAE investment has transformed swathes of the city.

"One of the prime drivers for building this partnership with the Abu Dhabi United Group [a private equity arm of the state mostly concerned with the football club] was that at that point in time, 2014, there was no other private investor willing to invest at the scale that the council wanted. And to do so in a way that created a build-to-rent market," she said.

The UAE investment in Manchester, accelerated by the Manchester Life Partnership with the city's council signed in 2014, has been a key defense for the City owners and fans when faced with sportswashing allegations in the past. And while Blakeley believes councils in the UK are often forced to seek foreign investment, there has been some pushback on how east Manchester has changed.

"A lot of people would be critical of some of the developments in [the districts of] Ancoats and New Islington and say that it's gentrified the area and there are some tensions between newer populations coming in and living in the more expensive flats and those already there. I think there's some truth to it. But I also think there's some truth to the fact that people forget really, really quickly that if you go back to just the 1990s, Ancoats was a no-go area," she said.

While profit for both council and state have certainly been a driver of the partnership, Blakeley thinks that forging greater links to the community could be something City's owners see "as a beneficial side effect."

After 18 years in the club and city, the UAE are deeply embedded in this part of Manchester. With political ties between the UK and UAE now reportedly strained as a result of the charges, there are suggestions that City's owners will try to sell the club. They may also be forced to do so, with sanctions still to be decided pending City's appeal.

There is no such trouble at PSG, who look capable of winning a third straight Champions League powered by their own Gulf-State coffers.

What is clear is that state-owned football clubs can become successful fast in a free market, as the Premier League was when the UAE owners arrived. Deep pockets, strong contacts at the top tables of the sport and canny strategies to integrate with the local area can be a heady mix.

It's certainly one that's proved intoxicating to City's fans, who brush off secret second contracts, sham sponsorship and unreliable witnesses with a loyalty built on success, community and investment. Judging by the mood in Manchester on Wednesday, no sanctions are going to change that.
