#397. Apple Unveils Its First-Ever MacBook Pro With a Touchscreen OLED

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Apple Unveils Its First-Ever MacBook Pro With a Touchscreen OLED

Apple has announced its first-ever touchscreen-equipped Macs. The redesigned MacBook Pro models are also Apple’s first laptops with OLED displays. It’s a big day for the Mac.

The MacBook Pro has always been controlled through its trackpad and keyboard. Apple has never made a touchscreen-equipped Mac before. While the latest iPad Pro and iPadOS 27 bring Apple’s tablet closer than ever to a Mac-like experience, a gap has long existed between the Mac and touchscreens. The gap has now officially closed.

“The new MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR Display is a monumental step forward for the Mac,” says Johny Srouji, Apple’s chief hardware officer.

“PROLED XDR is the world’s best laptop display. It pairs state-of-the-art tandem OLED technology with advanced algorithms in our display engine to deliver the kind of visual fidelity pros have been dreaming of. And with the magic of Touch coming to MacBook Pro, combined with the game-changing performance of M5 Pro and M5 Max, we’ve created a whole new way for pros to interact with their content, taking their workflows to a completely new level. The new MacBook Pro is the result of the deep integration between Apple silicon, hardware, and software, and there’s simply nothing else like it,” Srouji continues.

At a “Pro and Tell” event in Los Angeles today, Apple revealed arguably the most significant MacBook Pro update ever, and certainly the biggest change to the popular notebook series since Apple moved to its own Apple Silicon M-series chips in 2020. The new MacBook Pro, available in 14- and 16-inch models, marks a major shift for the MacBook Pro, which has largely kept the same form factor and functionality since the first MacBook Pro arrived 20 years ago.

At first glance, the redesigned MacBook Pro looks familiar. It also packs the familiar M5 Pro and M5 Max chips inside, which, as PetaPixel has seen, are incredible. However, that’s about where the similarities end.

The new MacBook Pro sports what Apple calls a “PROLED XDR” display, essentially an OLED “XDR”. It is a tandem OLED design with 2,000 nits of peak brightness, a 120Hz refresh rate, and the exceptionally black blacks that OLEDs are known for. Apple promises that the screen is effectively a professional-grade, portable reference monitor for creatives.

With the move to the new OLED touchscreen, the illuminated Mac logo on the back has returned, too.

Apple describes the PROLED XDR as “the world’s best display in a pro laptop.” The company specifically cites its effectiveness for photographers, colorists, and content creators. The super-wide color gamut supports P3 and now Adobe RGB workflows, and Apple promises better coverage in the green and cyan ranges in particular. It also offers more than 80% Rec. 2020 coverage for HDR video editing and color grading, according to Apple.

With up to 2,000 nits of peak SDR brightness, the new MacBook Pro should perform well outdoors. Those who require anti-glare performance can also opt for a nano-texture display. The screen also includes a Dynamic Island, like the iPhone, instead of the traditional notch. This serves as the new home for Spotlight, Live Activities, and Siri AI.

The bezels are also thinner, which means slightly larger screens without increasing the footprint. The 16-inch display is now 16.3 inches, while the 14-inch one is 14.2 inches diagonally.

Beyond the bright, sharp, high-contrast, and colorful display, it’s also obviously notable that it can be touched. Apple says this makes everyday interactions easier and more intuitive, like scrolling, swiping, and pinching elements on screen. Apple builds the touch sensor directly into the display panel, rather than layering it on top, which it says reduces latency.

“Touch also introduces entirely new ways of working for pro users. Graphic designers can now use the entire display as their canvas, no longer constrained by the area of the trackpad when drawing; musicians can play software instruments right onscreen; and app developers can test how Touch performs in their iOS and iPadOS apps directly on the MacBook Pro display,” Apple says. The MacBook Pro’s new touchscreen also works alongside existing keyboard and trackpad shortcuts.

Apple also says the newly designed hinge is stronger than before, so the screen shouldn’t wobble when you touch it. Apple also developed new video stabilization to keep FaceTime calls from getting shaky when the caller touches their screen.

Apple’s first-party apps are optimized for the new MacBook Pro straight out of the gate, and macOS automatically translates taps into clicks. However, third-party developers will want to use new touch APIs to make sure their apps take full advantage of the new MacBook Pro. Some apps are already optimized, though, including DaVinci Resolve and Adobe Lightroom.

The new MacBook Pro is also thinner and lighter. The 14-inch MacBook Pro now weighs half a pound less, tipping the scales at just three pounds. The 16-inch version is four pounds, 0.7 pounds lighter than before. Both models are slimmer thanks to the OLED’s thinner stack. It’s not a huge difference, though: the 14-inch is half a millimeter thinner, and the 16-inch is nearly a full millimeter slimmer.

Audio matters, too. An all-new woofer design promises 50% deeper bass, and new tweeters that Apple says improve sound clarity. For the first time in a MacBook Pro, Apple also added new audio tuning specifically for closed-lid use. Apple says the closed laptop will still deliver great audio, replacing the need for dedicated speakers when using external displays.

The M5 Pro and M5 Max chips inside are the same as prior Macs. That is, they are very powerful.

“It raises the bar for what was already the world’s best pro laptop. We love the Mac, and it’s the reason why so many of us found our way to Apple in the first place. The Mac has never been better, and it’s never been stronger,” Apple’s new CEO, John Ternus, says.

The new MacBook Pro has three Thunderbolt 5 ports, HDMI, and an SDXC card slot. The laptop supports up to three external displays with M5 Pro and up to four with M5 Max, plus the PROLED XDR.

The laptop includes Wi-Fi 7 and Bluetooth 6 connectivity. It can fast charge up to 50% in just 30 minutes and promises up to 24 hours of battery life.

The MacBook Pro also offers several other interesting features with PROLED XDR. A new Apple Pro Display Calibrator lets users calibrate the built-in display to very specific targets. A new Apple Color Matching Function 2026 lets the PROLED XDR perfectly match an iPhone 18 Pro or Studio Display XDR.

The new MacBook Pro starts at $2,899 for the 14-inch model with M5 Pro and $3,399 for the 16-inch one with M5 Pro. The M5 Pro models start with 24GB of unified memory and expand to 64GB. Storage starts at 1TB, expandable to 4TB.

They are also available with the M5 Max. The 14-inch starts at $4,449, and the M5 Max starts at $4,799. These start with an 18-core CPU and a 32-core GPU. An upgraded 40-core GPU is also available for $300. These machines start with 36GB of unified memory and 2TB SSDs, and can be expanded to 128GB of RAM and, for the first time, 16TB of internal storage.

For reference, a maxed-out 16-inch MacBook Pro with 128GB of unified memory and 16TB of storage is $14,549. A nano-texture screen is an extra $150.

The new MacBook Pro is available to preorder now and releases on November 6.

#353. UAE: UN Secretary-General Candidate Whitewashed Abuses

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image Lana Zaki Nusseibeh, representative to the United Nations of the United Arab Emirates, speaks during a UN Security Council meeting at UN headquarters on February 23, 2023. © 2023 John Minchillo/AP Photo

(Beirut) – Lana Zaki Nusseibeh, a long-serving UAE diplomat and foreign affairs official, has an extensive record of denying and whitewashing the country’s rights abuses in Sudan and Yemen, Human Rights Watch said today. On October 21, 2026, the UAE formally nominated Nusseibeh to be the new United Nations secretary-general.

“For years, Lana Nusseibeh has used her position to vehemently deny and divert attention from the UAE’s war crimes in Yemen and support for the abusive Rapid Support Forces in Sudan,” said Bruno Stagno Ugarte, chief advocacy officer at Human Rights Watch. “Her disinformation about the UAE’s rights record, particularly her dismissal of the UN’s own reports documenting the UAE’s links to the RSF, has seriously undermined justice and accountability for the victims.”

Nusseibeh has repeatedly denied that the UAE provides military support to the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) in Sudan, while refusing to address clear evidence to the contrary. The RSF, engaged in a conflict with the Sudan Armed Forces (SAF), is responsible for widespread war crimes and crimes against humanity, including as part of a campaign of ethnic cleansing in the Darfur region. The United Nations Fact-Finding Mission on Sudan concluded that their assault on the North Darfur capital of El Fasher “bore the hallmarks of genocide.”

In September 2024, Nusseibeh said in a letter to the editor of The Economist that, “[T]he UAE is not providing weapons or any other support to the Rapid Support Forces or any of the warring parties in Sudan.” The letter followed the publication of the first UN Panel of Experts on Sudan’s report, which included conclusions of “credible allegations” that the UAE was providing military support to the RSF through Chad. A UAE government letter from December 2023 in the report annex claims that flights from the UAE to Chad transported humanitarian assistance to Sudanese civilians. The letter is unsigned but bears the letterhead of the Permanent Mission of the UAE to the UN in New York. Nusseibeh was the head of the UAE’s mission from 2013 until April 2024.

In April 2024, in response to accusations from the government of Sudan that the UAE was providing the RSF with military, logistical, and political support, Nusseibeh said in a statement that the “claims were baseless” and affirmed the UAE’s categorical rejection “of the false allegations.” In July 2024, asked by CNN whether the UAE was arming the RSF, Nusseibeh replied: “No, those allegations are categorically untrue. The UAE, as it has done in countless places around the world when there is a humanitarian crisis, has projected humanitarian aid in as quickly as possible and then scaled that way.”

In November 2025, as a minister of state, Nusseibeh took part in an Emirati “lobbying blitz” with key European Parliament members that said that the UAE was “playing a constructive role in Sudan” in the lead up to a vote on a resolution condemning atrocities in Sudan, according to Politico. The European Parliament passed the resolution without mention of the UAE’s role in the conflict. Several parliamentary sources told Euronews that Nusseibeh “personally lobbied MEPs [members of the European Parliament] in Strasbourg to influence the content of the resolution.”

In a subsequent resolution adopted in July 2026, the European Parliament called out the UAE’s role in support of the RSF and requested sanctions on a UAE company involved in the hiring and transfer of mercenaries to the group.

A May 2026 Human Rights Watch report found that Colombian private military contractors, apparently hired by a UAE-based company, transited through UAE military bases before being deployed to Sudan to support and fight alongside the RSF, notably during the forces’ takeover of El Fasher, during which the RSF committed multiple massacres, widespread sexual violence, and a massive abduction-for-ransom racket. The findings provide further evidence that the UAE has been assisting or otherwise substantially contributing to the RSF’s capacity to commit war crimes.

In August, the Fact-Finding Mission on Sudan released a report echoing these findings, concluding “that individuals and entities operating from, registered in, or linked to the United Arab Emirates played a significant role in networks involved in the recruitment, financing, transportation and deployment of foreign military contractors and the provision of military-related support benefiting the Rapid Support Forces.” The mission noted “several sources” indicating the presence of Colombian fighters in and around El Fasher before, during, and after its takeover. 

Human Rights Watch has also documented the RSF using military equipment—Serbian-made shells equipping an attack drone—that had first been sold to benefit the UAE armed forces.

On October 27, during an interactive dialogue with UN member states and civil society organizations, Nusseibeh was asked about her denials of findings by the Panel of Experts on Sudan and the Fact-Finding Mission. She did not respond directly to a question about whether she would “continue to reject the findings of the very organization [she] seeks to lead.” Instead, she stated that she had taken a leave of absence from her post as a UAE minister and would refer any questions about the UAE to the UAE government.

As permanent representative to the UN, Nusseibeh repeatedly defended the UAE as complying with international law during its intervention in Yemen while dismissing evidence of widely documented abuses. Beginning in March 2015, the UAE played a prominent role in a coalition of countries that conducted military operations in Yemen, including by committing over 30 fighter jets to carry out airstrikes, providing naval ships to enforce the coalition’s maritime blockade, and supporting abusive local forces.

In 2016, Human Rights Watch documented 58 unlawful Saudi/UAE-coalition airstrikes, killing nearly 800 civilians and hitting homes, markets, hospitals, schools, businesses, and mosques, and the coalition's use of cluster munitions.

In August 2016 and May 2018, Nusseibeh issued statements defending UAE actions in Yemen, claiming that the UAE “takes its own responsibility for upholding international humanitarian law and for protecting civilians very seriously.” The 2018 statement was made a few months before the UAE ordered humanitarian organizations in the coastal city of Hodeida to evacuate despite the immense humanitarian needs, while the Saudi/UAE-led coalition sought to take control of the city from the Houthis. 

In 2021, the UAE, alongside Saudi Arabia, successfully led a lobbying campaign to end the mandate of the Group of Eminent Experts, an international, impartial, and independent body the Human Rights Council had established to report on rights violations and abuses in Yemen.

“Lana Nusseibeh has spent over a decade in roles acting for the UAE government and repeatedly whitewashing their abusive role in Yemen and Sudan, presenting Abu Dhabi as a humanitarian actor while dismissing or obscuring mounting evidence of its role in fueling abuses,” Stagno Ugarte said. “Having dismissed and undermined the findings of the UN’s own reports, UN member states should be asking tough questions about Nusseibeh’s willingness to defend the independence and integrity of the UN and its investigative mechanisms, including those exposing UAE abuses, at a time when these institutions are under attack.”
