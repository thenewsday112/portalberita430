#412. Can Community Programs Reduce Gun Violence?

A new study by researchers at UC Berkeley’s Center for Global Healthy Cities published in the Journal of the American Medical Association Network Open found that the California city of Richmond’s Office of Neighborhood Safety (ONS) was responsible for a 61% reduction in that city’s firearm homicides and injury shootings over the last 14 years.

This is one of the only studies in the country to document the sustained impacts of a community violence intervention program on city-wide firearm violence over more than 10 years.

According to lead author Alice Bruno, a doctoral student in epidemiology at UC Berkeley’s School of Public Health, “we leveraged data from dozens of comparable cities across the United States to estimate what firearm violence would have looked like in Richmond without the ONS, Peacemaker Fellowship. We found that the Fellowship contributed to long-term reductions in violence, and that the declines only grew as the program operated.” Other UC Berkeley coauthors include Jason Corburn; Mahasin Mujahid, chair of the Division of Epidemiology; Sean Darling-Hammond; and Juan Cabrera.

Sam Vaughn, director of ONS, said, “The statistics back up what we have seen in the streets: fewer shootings, fewer deaths, and a healthier community overall.”

Homicide rates are down across the country; the FBI recently released data showing murder rates are down a record 18.1% nationally. However, Richmond has consistently outperformed national declines for over a decade, suggesting the results are due to that city’s innovative, street-outreach interventions and their mentorship of active firearm offenders, a program called Operation Peacemaker Fellowship, which was instituted in 2010. The Fellowship uses a public health approach to preventing gun violence.

According to James Houston, the program manager of the ONS who oversees the day-to-day implementation of the Peacemaker Fellowship, including managing 14 street-outreach workers who currently serve 123 participants, “the credit goes to the young people making better decisions and the team that gives them support, mentorship, and encouragement every day to make a change.”

According to Shasa Curl, Richmond city manager, Richmond had over 300 police officer full-time equivalent in 2014 and 180 in 2020, but was down to 121 active sworn officers by 2024. Yet during this time, the ONS, through continued city support and grants, was able to expand its staff and increase the number of fellows and participants. Even during the COVID-19 period, the study shows Richmond did not have as significant a spike in firearm violence as most cities did across the Bay Area and nationally.

According to the ONS, the health impacts are lives saved, safer streets, and community members with a renewed positive life outlook.

One former Peacemaker Fellowship participant said, “Before the ONS came to me, I didn’t care if I survived past 19, as long as I had a name in the streets. After my experience with the ONS, with my mentor coming at me every day, I got support for my stress, got a job, paying my bills, and have a kid now. I’m no longer terrorizing my community.”

The paper notes the cascading health benefits for entire communities when gun violence reductions are sustained, such as the case in Richmond. Further research will explore how unique aspects of the ONS might be contributing to declines in gun violence, including that the program is a city government agency, independent from the police department, has had consistent funding, has trained and retained many of the same outreach workers for over a decade (these city staff are well paid with benefits), and delivers individualized supports to each participant based on their needs, such as food and housing assistance, cognitive behavioral therapy, addiction and anger management counseling, and other social services aimed at both reducing gun violence risk and addressing trauma.

#318. Michael Strahan Shares the Words That Helped His Daughter Through Brain Cancer Treatment

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

  • Michael Strahan opened up to Healthline about his daughter Isabella’s brain cancer diagnosis and how the experience strengthened their relationship.
  • After undergoing surgery, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy for medulloblastoma, Isabella has returned to college and is embracing life after treatment.
  • Strahan shared how he helped his daughter maintain hope during treatment and why they’re speaking out to support other families affected by cancer.

For years, Michael Strahan appeared in commercials for St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital, delivering a message he never imagined would become so personal: “No parent should have to hear that their child has cancer.”

But in 2023, the beloved TV host and former NFL player faced that reality when his daughter, Isabella, was diagnosed with brain cancer at 19 years old.

“[That] line that I used to deliver, usually at the end of the commercial…here I am actually filling that line in my life in real time,” Strahan told Healthline.

Strahan believes his longtime connection with St. Jude was more than a coincidence. After years of advocating for families affected by childhood cancer, he found himself on the other side of that experience.

“When I needed that family…they were there for me,” he said. “I’m just appreciative, and I think things come back to you; give out good, good comes back.”

Facing brain cancer at 19 years old

In October 2023, Isabella began experiencing vomiting, headaches, and balance issues during her freshman year at the University of Southern California (USC). Tests revealed that she had medulloblastoma, a fast-growing cancerous brain tumor located in the cerebellum. She underwent emergency brain surgery, radiation therapy, and four rounds of chemotherapy.

While medulloblastoma typically occurs in children between the ages of 3 and 9, it can also affect young adults like Isabella.

“[The] biology in adolescents and young adults can differ meaningfully from pediatric cases, requiring specialized expertise at centers of excellence for this disease,” Arnab Chakravarti, MD, director of the Brain Tumor Program at The Ohio State University, told Healthline.

Medulloblastoma is sometimes confused with glioblastoma, the most common type of malignant primary brain tumor in adults. Glioblastoma is particularly aggressive, with a median overall survival of just 15 months and a 5-year survival rate of less than 10%.

However, Chakravarti explained that medulloblastoma has a significantly higher cure rate than glioblastoma.

“[This is] a point of genuine hope that deserves to be communicated clearly,” he said.

Radiation therapy is a cornerstone of medulloblastoma treatment. Isabella received proton therapy, which uses precisely targeted radiation to treat tumors while limiting exposure to surrounding healthy tissue.

“For medulloblastoma, this physical precision is transformative,” Chakravarti said. “Proton craniospinal irradiation dramatically reduces [collateral] exposure — preserving cochlear function and hearing, reducing cardiovascular risk, protecting the thyroid, and most critically, sparing developing brain tissue in ways that translate into measurably better neurocognitive outcomes years after treatment.”

He added that multiple prospective studies have shown that children and young adults treated with proton craniospinal irradiation experience less cognitive decline and better quality of life compared to those treated with conventional radiation.

Today, Isabella is back at USC, embracing the everyday routines she once had to put on hold.

“I am back at USC, so…it’s been great, in the midst of exams, so it’s a little stressful, but I actually [am] inviting the stress in because at one point I didn’t have exams, so I’m happy to be back,” Isabella told Healthline.

Along with attending classes, Isabella is enjoying activities such as working out and cooking. She continues to undergo MRI scans of her head and spine every three months, but said that after another year, the scans will become less frequent, occurring every six months.

“But really…I don’t have a lot of doctor’s appointments,” she said.

Her outlook on life has also changed.

“It feels like a brand-new life, but it’s a good one. And that’s wonderful,” Isabella said.

Cancer strengthened their father-daughter bond

Isabella said her family’s support played an important role in helping her through cancer treatment. Spending so much time together in the hospital brought them closer as they watched movies and played cards and board games to pass the time.

“[It] really, really bonds you to people, and just having my dad and my whole family there was something that [you] can’t beat,” she told Healthline.

Michael said that despite the difficult circumstances, he cherishes the time he spent with his daughter. Before her diagnosis, Isabella was a typical teenager who spent much of her time at school or with friends.

“They say you spend the most time with your kid for the first few years of their life, and then once school starts, you don’t get a chance to spend as much time as you would like with them,” he said.

While Michael said he and Isabella already had a close relationship, her diagnosis brought the family even closer and helped them reflect on what mattered most.

“[What] was really important was family, and relationships, and the love that you have for each other, and the support you have to give each other,” he said. “I think that our relationship now is better than ever.”

Today, Michael makes an effort to visit Isabella at USC whenever he’s in Los Angeles during the NFL season.

“Some weekends she [has time for me]. Some she doesn’t,” he said. “But just to have that relationship with your child, where they want to hang out with you, is a good feeling, and that’s the relationship that we have.”

Turning experience into advocacy

Michael and Isabella are sharing their story to raise awareness about brain cancer and offer hope to other families facing similar challenges.

They recently participated in a fireside chat in New York City hosted by Real Chemistry and SMAC, where they discussed their family’s experience navigating Isabella’s diagnosis, mental health, caregiving, and the impact of sharing their story.

Throughout her treatment, Isabella also documented her experience through a series of YouTube vlogs and the ABC primetime documentary, “Life Interrupted: Isabella Strahan’s Fight to Beat Cancer.”

For Michael, helping others is a value he learned from his parents. He recalled how his father used his transportation business in Germany to support people in need after retiring from the military.

“[He] would take donations, and use his money and his trucks, and drive stuff to Yugoslavia and Czechoslovakia, and drop it off there at local churches and the shelters for people, who he had no connection with,” said Michael. “So for me, I think I do what I feel is on my heart and what’s important to me.”

Why Michael Strahan believes hope matters when facing cancer

For parents caring for children with cancer, Michael emphasized that it’s OK to feel overwhelmed or emotional. He encouraged them to show their children love and support while helping them maintain hope for the future.

“I think positivity is the main thing because it was so tough on her at the time when she was [saying], ‘I can’t do it. I can’t do this. I can’t do that,’” said Michael.

Michael said he made a conscious effort to replace words like “can’t” and “if” with “when” while encouraging Isabella during treatment.

“Anytime she got down on herself, I’d say, ‘when you go back to college, when you do this, when you do that,’” said Michael. “And I just think the positive attitude and the love that you can give and the confidence that you can display towards your loved one [or] your child who’s going through something like this is the only thing you can do because you’re at the mercy of medicine.”

Now in her senior year at USC, Isabella is looking ahead to life after graduation.

“[There’s] a lot of interesting opportunities and things that I want to pursue,” she said.

Michael has no doubt she will find her way.

“She’s so talented, and this experience, in my opinion, has opened up my eyes to more talent that she has that I didn’t realize…the talent to connect people, and be in front of a crowd. I always thought she’d be nervous because I get nervous, but she’s better than me,” he said.

#19. Manchester City, the UAE and the UK's OM in a political tangle

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Nearly 20 years after arriving in England, Manchester City's UAE owners could exit amid cheating charges. That would pose a challenge for a city — and prime minister — closely tied to Abu Dhabi.

Defiance was the word that stood out as Manchester City returned home for the first time since they were found guilty of 114 financial charges by the Premier League in a case that's made headlines around the world.

City showed a measure of it on the pitch, even as they lost 4-2 to holders, Qatari-owned Paris Saint-Germain, in the Champions League group stages on Wednesday.

Among the home fanbase at the Etihad Stadium, DW found universal support for City's Abu Dhabi owners — headed by Sheikh Mansour, who is also UAE's vice president and deputy prime minister.

"I'm all in with the ownership now," said local fan Matt. "If we end up back in League Two [fourth tier], so be it. We've got 15 years of memories we wouldn't have otherwise and to me it's just an accounting line on a spreadsheet really."

The stadium name itself is a reminder of the takeover that changed this club, and its part of Manchester, in 2008. The national airline of the UAE, Etihad's inflated sponsorship of City is among the key planks of the case against them, while many buildings and businesses around the stadium bear the brand. It's a stadium that's also seen plenty of success of late.

"We've seen things other fans will never see," added fellow City fan Josh. "I think people have got to consider how many people this club has given great memories to, how many people's lives have been changed by it, myself included....you've got to be grateful for that."

Though many in sky blue shared those sentiments, most outside the club now view the trophies won in the relevant period as tarnished. On top of that, critics point to City's reticence in providing evidence in their case, the long-documented human rights abuses in UAE and the often dismissive tone with which the owners have dealt with being found guilty of charges they have now appealed.

But those City fans appear to have one powerful ally in British Prime Minister Andy Burnham. Though he has since sought to tone down his position on Mansour and co. somewhat, the former mayor of Greater Manchester said he "would be really concerned to lose them," after the verdict was announced in late September. "They've been such a huge partner in the building of modern Manchester," he added.

While Burnham has since denied being close to City's ownership, even as City were playing on Wednesday, The Guardian reported a club representative was invited on a government-backed trade delegation to India this week by Burnham's successor as Manchester mayor, Bev Craig.

Earlier the same day, Burnham was forced to confirm he had two additional dinners with the club chair, Khaldoon Al Mubarak, at the Etihad Stadium, having previously declared those only as matchday hospitality. It has become a serious domestic issue for the man who replaced Keir Starmer in July.

What is clear is that Burnham welcomed Abu Dhabi investment during his time in charge of the northwestern city. This came mainly in the form of residential and commercial buildings as well as libraries and sports centers, all popping up in previously run-down areas in and around the stadium.

Georgina Blakeley, a Professor at the University of Huddersfield who wrote a book called "The regeneration of east Manchester," told DW that UAE investment has transformed swathes of the city.

"One of the prime drivers for building this partnership with the Abu Dhabi United Group [a private equity arm of the state mostly concerned with the football club] was that at that point in time, 2014, there was no other private investor willing to invest at the scale that the council wanted. And to do so in a way that created a build-to-rent market," she said.

The UAE investment in Manchester, accelerated by the Manchester Life Partnership with the city's council signed in 2014, has been a key defense for the City owners and fans when faced with sportswashing allegations in the past. And while Blakeley believes councils in the UK are often forced to seek foreign investment, there has been some pushback on how east Manchester has changed.

"A lot of people would be critical of some of the developments in [the districts of] Ancoats and New Islington and say that it's gentrified the area and there are some tensions between newer populations coming in and living in the more expensive flats and those already there. I think there's some truth to it. But I also think there's some truth to the fact that people forget really, really quickly that if you go back to just the 1990s, Ancoats was a no-go area," she said.

While profit for both council and state have certainly been a driver of the partnership, Blakeley thinks that forging greater links to the community could be something City's owners see "as a beneficial side effect."

After 18 years in the club and city, the UAE are deeply embedded in this part of Manchester. With political ties between the UK and UAE now reportedly strained as a result of the charges, there are suggestions that City's owners will try to sell the club. They may also be forced to do so, with sanctions still to be decided pending City's appeal.

There is no such trouble at PSG, who look capable of winning a third straight Champions League powered by their own Gulf-State coffers.

What is clear is that state-owned football clubs can become successful fast in a free market, as the Premier League was when the UAE owners arrived. Deep pockets, strong contacts at the top tables of the sport and canny strategies to integrate with the local area can be a heady mix.

It's certainly one that's proved intoxicating to City's fans, who brush off secret second contracts, sham sponsorship and unreliable witnesses with a loyalty built on success, community and investment. Judging by the mood in Manchester on Wednesday, no sanctions are going to change that.

#358. Yemen: Houthi Attack on Taizz Road Threatens Lives, Welfare

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image The Hayjat al-Abd road, which connects Aden to Taizz city, Yemen, September 26, 2026. © 2026 Photo by AFP via Getty Images

(Beirut) – The Houthi armed group on October 18, 2026, used explosives to severely damage a critical road into Yemen’s besieged city of Taizz, putting civilian lives and welfare at increased risk, Human Rights Watch said today. 

The Hayjat al-Abd road has been the main entry point for food, medicine, and fuel to reach residents of Taizz city, which the Houthis have besieged since 2015. Fighting in the area has primarily been between the Houthis and forces aligned with Yemen’s internationally recognized government. The Houthis’ deliberate destruction of segments of the road, if intended to disable infrastructure indispensable to civilian survival, would violate the laws of war and amount to a war crime. The attack may also have caused disproportionate civilian harm.  

“The Houthi attack on the remaining commercial supply route to besieged Taizz further endangers civilians already struggling with severe shortages of food, water, medicine, and fuel,” said Niku Jafarnia, Yemen and Bahrain researcher at Human Rights Watch. “If Taizz residents’ access along this vital route is cut off for a prolonged period, they could face even greater risks to their lives and well-being.” 

Human Rights Watch spoke to eight informed sources in October about two attacks on the road and their aftermath, including civil society workers and a relative of a witness to the October 18 attack. Human Rights Watch also analyzed satellite imagery and verified photographs and a video from sources or posted on social media. In 2024, Human Rights Watch spoke with six Taizz residents about the difficulty of meeting basic needs during the Houthi siege. 

Human Rights Watch wrote to the Houthis on October 21 about attacks on the road but did not receive a response.

Control over Taizz has been divided since 2015, when Houthi forces entered the governorate and surrounded its capital, Taizz city, Yemen’s third largest city. The city has remained under Yemeni government control, but the Houthis have cut off vital roads connecting it to the rest of the country, notably the port city of Aden, the Yemeni government’s current capital. 

While the laws of war do not prohibit sieges directed at military objectives, besieging forces are prohibited from using starvation of civilians as a method of warfare and must allow and facilitate humanitarian relief. Human Rights Watch has found that the Houthis’ blocking of roads into Taizz has severely restricted civilians’ ability to move in and out of the city and impeded the flow of essential goods, including water, and humanitarian access to the civilian population. 

Click to expand Image Graphic © 2026 Human Rights Watch

At about 4:00 a.m. on October 18, Houthi forces detonated explosives on the Hayjat al-Abd road in the town of al-Turba, significantly damaging a portion of the road. Residents in the area as well as two other informed sources said that the Houthis had been in control of al-Turba, about 75 kilometers south of Taizz city, since October 4. Since then, there had been no ground fighting in the area, including on the night of the attack. Nor had there been any indications that government forces or other armed groups were trying to re-enter the area, indicating that there was not another group that could have carried out the attack. 

Another source said his relative told him: “It was a massive and terrifying explosion—everyone woke up. Rocks reached all the way to my house.” 

Human Rights Watch verified and geolocated four photographs and a video from sources showing a damaged culvert on the switchbacks of the Hayjat al-Abd road. The damage is consistent with a subsurface detonation of explosives. 

Two weeks earlier, apparent Houthi forces had attacked the Hayjat al-Abd road about 85 kilometers southeast of Taizz city. A civil society worker in Taizz who spoke with several people knowledgeable about the attack said that on the night and early morning of September 30-October 1, Houthi forces and Yemeni government-aligned forces fought on several fronts around Taizz. The sources said that Houthi forces infiltrated and reached the Hayjat al-Abd road, placing and detonating explosives in a culvert under the road just past Souq al-Wahati, a local market. The Houthi forces then withdrew. 

Two other sources gave similar accounts based on communications with local residents. Human Rights Watch was unable to conclusively determine that Houthi forces carried out the attack. 

Human Rights Watch verified and geolocated two additional photographs posted on social media on October 1 showing a damaged culvert on the Hayjat al-Abd road at the village of al-Muharraqah, where the road starts ascending to Taizz city. The damage to the culvert in the images is consistent with a subsurface detonation of explosives. Satellite imagery captured at 6:57 a.m. on October 1 shows damage to the culvert across the full width of the road. 

Government forces quickly retook the area after the first attack and later filled the destroyed portion of the road with dirt to allow vehicles to cross. Two sources said that government authorities had largely closed down the road to avoid further attacks on vehicles, though some vehicles continued to pass through. 

Since the October 1 attack, all roads into and out of Taizz have largely been blocked by Yemeni government-affiliated and Houthi forces due to hostilities. However, before the second attack, some supplies, including flour, had still been taken to Taizz city on the Hayjat al-Abd road, said two civil society workers in the area.

However, the United Nations World Food Programme (WFP) said on October 14 that they had not been able to access the “more than 600,000 people in Taiz who depend on humanitarian assistance,” which was “especially alarming” since most of Taizz’s districts are considered to have emergency levels of hunger. The WFP also said that “wheat flour stocks are nearly exhausted” in the city and that “remaining food supplies in local markets may last only a short time if access constraints continue.”

Under international humanitarian law, roads are presumptively civilian objects that become military objectives if by their nature, location, purpose, or use, they make an effective contribution to military action and their destruction, capture, or neutralization provides a definite military advantage at the time. Even if the Hayjat al-Abd road qualifies as a military objective, an attack would be unlawfully disproportionate if the expected incidental civilian harm would be excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated. Given the road’s critical role in Taizz city civilians’ access to food, water, medicine, and other basic needs, the assessment should account for the reasonably foreseeable consequences of damaging or closing it. 

The Houthis should have known, particularly given the UN’s repeated warnings, that supplies in Taizz were running out and that further closures after October 1 from attacks on the road could have grave effects for besieged Taizz civilians relying on the road for necessities.

International humanitarian law also prohibits attacking, destroying, or rendering useless objects indispensable to the survival of a civilian population. The October 18 attack disrupted the remaining major commercial supply route to Taizz city. Whether it violated this prohibition depends on whether it was carried out for the purpose of denying civilians access to objects indispensable to their survival. 

Serious violations of the laws of war ordered or committed with criminal intent—that is, deliberately or recklessly—are war crimes.

“The fighting in Taizz could have dire consequences for the hundreds of thousands of civilians in need of aid,” Jafarnia said. “All warring parties should take care to protect civilians in Yemen who have already experienced considerable suffering over 12 years of conflict.”

The exceptions that have applied to Leverkusen and Wolfsburg are under review.

The bedrock of our football culture is 50+1. This rule exists only in Germany, yet it speaks to a spirit that fits perfectly with the European sports model: 50+1 maintains a balance; on the one hand it allows for investment, on the other it guarantees participation. It strengthens the rights of members and fans. Perhaps it is because of this rule that the average attendance in Germany is higher than anywhere else in the world.

Mexico City runs on fondas and street food. These casual eateries serve homestyle food that’s quick, affordable, and feels like a warm hug. Stepping inside a fonda means being a part of a daily ritual known as comida corrida, where a meal typically unfolds in four acts: a steaming bowl of soup or broth to start, followed by your choice of rice or pasta. Next comes the main course, which at some places, like Nuevo Tehuacán, is what dictates the price of the meal; meatballs in chipotle, pork with tomatillo and purslane, and chicken in peanut sauce are classics. Everything is washed down with a pitcher of freshly made agua fresca and finished with a sweet treat like a cup of gelatin. Look around, and you’ll see schoolchildren sitting elbow to elbow with office workers all savoring the comfort of one of the city’s most democratic meals.

Mostly homemade, flan is a staple of casual Mexican cooking, but it’s one of those desserts that look deceptively simple. Getting the texture just right (silky, firm, not overly sweet) is way harder than it seems, which may be why the best versions are often found in homes. Siembra may be a temple dedicated to heirloom corn masa, tacos, and antojitos, but its flan has all the right qualifications to receive just as much attention.

Ice cream parlors used to be an almost daily afternoon ritual for families and couples alike. Nevería Roxy was one of the early ones, and its 1950s-diner palate of teal and red has barely changed, even in the newer locations. Alongside the classic milk- and cream-based flavors, sundaes, floats, and banana splits, you’ll find nieves.

The word literally translates to “snow,” and the closest comparison might be a sorbet, though the texture tends to be a little coarser and the flavors typically center around seasonal fruits. Mamey, an endemic fruit, may have a short season, but Roxy keeps its nieve de mamey year-round. With a faintly nutty flavor reminiscent of baked sweet potato, it’s become a local favorite. And thanks to mamey’s lush, natural creaminess, it’s easy to forget there’s no dairy in the cup.

#330. Madrid 5-Day Itinerary: A Guide to Culture, Gastronomy, and Local Life

📅 May 06, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Home to some 3.5 million people, Madrid is the capital of Spain and the country’s largest city. Though it’s somewhat sprawling, the city boasts stunning architecture, incredible food, and an energetic nightlife that stretches until dawn. Over 10 million people visit the city each year, and the minute you step out to explore, you’ll understand why Madrid is one of Europe’s top destinations.

Like Barcelona, Madrid is a city that starts late and goes slow. It’s a city for night owls, where dinner starts late and the parties even later. But if you can embrace the laid-back pace of life, the slow meals, and the late-night vibes, you’ll have a more authentic visit and uncover the true beating heart of the city’s culture.

I love all my visits to Madrid. I’ve been a few times over the years and the city never ceases to amaze me. The food, the energy, the art (so much art!). Madrid is just cool.

Being such a big city, I know it can be easy to feel lost. Where do you start with what you want to do? Where do you go? It took me a few visits to figure it out.

In this post, I’ll help you plan an epic five-day visit to Madrid that will ensure you not only see the main sights but that you also get off the beaten path and see just what makes Madrid so popular — and all without breaking the bank either!

This first day focuses on a prestigious area designated as a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2021. There are over 40 monuments and over 40 buildings on the site. It’s easily one of the most picturesque and historic areas of the city.

The route begins at the Prado Museum, one of the best museums on the planet. Here you’ll find works from El Greco, Velázquez, and Goya, as well as many other masters (such as Rubens, Botticelli, and Caravaggio to name a few). If you only visit one museum in Madrid, this is the one. It’s incredible and worth taking the time to explore. It’s one of the biggest museums in the world, too. Make sure you spend a few hours here at a minimum.

Just a 10-minute walk along the Paseo del Prado, the journey continues at the Thyssen-Bornemisza Museum, a private collection spanning from the Renaissance to Pop Art, and culminates at the Reina Sofía Museum. The Reina Sofía National Art Center offers a fantastic collection of modern art. Works by masters like Picasso, Miró, Kandinsky, Dalí, and Bacon can all be found here. The centerpiece is Picasso’s famous Guernica, a 1937 oil painting considered the most moving anti-war painting in the world. While I don’t love modern art, even I enjoyed this museum.

After museum hopping, it’s time to dive into Madrid’s culinary scene. Try Taberna La Fragua de Vulcano for a rustic atmosphere perfect for sampling cocido madrileño. If you are near the Reina Sofía Museum, don’t miss the vibrant garden terrace at Arzábal, renowned for having some of the best croquetas in the city. Finally, for a unique setting, Bodega de los Secretos lets you dine in restored 17th-century wine caves, making it the most atmospheric choice near the Prado Museum.

After a late lunch, spend some time exploring Madrid’s unique ‘House Museums. These are former private palaces that preserve the intimate atmosphere of their original owners. The crown jewel of this collection is the Sorolla Museum, the sun-drenched former residence of ‘the painter of light.’ Following a major architectural renovation, this underrated museum is set to reopen its doors in 2026, offering a refreshed look at its stunning gardens and Mediterranean canvases.

Or if you want something else, there is also the nearby Museum of Romanticism, where you can enjoy a coffee in its secret garden, or visit the Lázaro Galdiano Museum to marvel at a private collection of Goya, Murillo, Velázquez, El Greco, or Bosch housed in a magnificent neo-Renaissance mansion.

In the evening, enjoy some food and relaxation.

The second day delves into the medieval and Renaissance layout of the city, famously known as Habsburg Madrid (Madrid de los Austrias). The central hub is Puerta del Sol, where a photo at Kilometer Zero and the Bear and the Strawberry Tree statue are mandatory for a traveler’s geographic check-in. Kilometer Zero is the official starting point of all the highways and railways in the country, making this effectively the center of the entire country. There’s a small plaque you can snap a photo of.

Nearby is the Bear and the Strawberry Tree statue (El Oso y el Madroño). The statue shows a bear reaching up to eat fruit from a madroño tree, symbols which come from Madrid’s coat of arms (the bear represents the city and the tree represents the land and nature surrounding it that has helped make it prosper).

From here, walk toward the iconic Plaza Mayor, highlighting its porticoed architecture and the Casa de la Panadería. Plaza Mayor once hosted bullfights, markets, symphonies, tournaments, and even executions. Now it’s ringed with tourist shops, cafés, and restaurants. It’s a good place from which to indulge in some people-watching, offers some good (though overpriced) bars, and occasionally has concerts and events.

For a true “Local Life” experience, stop for a calamari sandwich in the nearby alleys. Your taste buds will thank you.

After a relaxing lunch and some people watching, head to the Royal Palace of Madrid. The largest palace in Western Europe, the Baroque-style palace was built in the 18th century and is home to over 3,400 lavishly decorated rooms. It was originally built on a former 9th-century Muslim fort and today functions much like an art museum with numerous works, including pieces by Caravaggio, Juan de Flandes, and Francisco Goya. Though it’s the official residence of the royal family, they don’t actually live here anymore, and the palace is only used for official state functions. You can walk through the palace (both self-guided and guided tours are available), starting at the grand stairway, and wander through lavishly decorated staterooms. There’s also the Royal Armory, which houses a collection of medieval weapons and armor.

To end the day, head to the Almudena Cathedral. Unlike many of the famous medieval churches in Europe, this one was constructed in the late 19th century. It was finally completed in 1993 and was consecrated by Pope John Paul II. While the exterior has a Neo-Classical design (to match the nearby palace), the cathedral also has a Neo-Romanesque crypt with over 400 columns that you can explore. Be sure to head up to the dome for stunning views out over the city.

Madrid is a paradise for foodies. It’s one of the reasons I keep coming back. While there are plenty of food tours to take in Madrid, I suggest heading off on your own to explore. Begin at the city’s iconic gastronomic markets. The glass-walled Mercado de San Miguel is a must for gourmet tapas near Plaza Mayor. It’s popular with the after-work crowd and is a place I usually visit a few times on my trips. Additionally, local favorites like Mercado de San Antón in Chueca and Mercado de Vallehermoso offer a deeper dive into regional flavors. Just make sure to bring an appetite.

If you do want to take a food tour, consider doing a food tour. This post has more of my suggestions.

For dinner, head to Sobrino de Botín, officially recognized by the Guinness World Records as the world’s oldest restaurant. It was actually a favorite of Ernest Hemingway during his time in Madrid. For a more casual vibe, go ‘tapas crawling’ through the historic streets of La Latina or Barrio de las Letras.

As the sun sets, head to the Gran Vía to experience Madrid’s world-class rooftop culture. Venues like The Roof at Picalagartos or RIU, 360º sky bar offer signature cocktails and dinner with breathtaking 360-degree views of the skyline.

For those who don’t eat meat, Madrid has actually evolved into one of Europe’s best veg hubs, boasting a vibrant vegetarian and vegan scene in neighborhoods like Malasaña, where plant-based versions of traditional Spanish dishes are now a staple of the city’s diverse culinary identity.

Madrid is the only European capital where nature, science, and royal history converge in a UNESCO World Heritage oasis known as the ‘Paisaje de la Luz‘ (Landscape of Light). But this prestigious area, encompassing the iconic El Retiro Park and the Paseo del Prado, is just the beginning of Spain’s monumental legacy. For more UNESCO wonders, take a day trip out of the city.

One popular destination worth visiting is the Monastery of San Lorenzo de El Escorial, a sprawling imperial masterpiece recognized by UNESCO for its historical significance. El Escorial is located just one hour from the city by car and was commissioned by Philip II in the 16th century. It’s a massive palatial complex that includes a monastery, library, palace, basilica, and tomb for Spanish monarchs. Yet as massive as it is, it feels more austere than other palatial complexes, likely owing to its granite walls and symmetrical layout.

Another option for a day trip is the Royal Palace and Gardens of Aranjuez, a World Heritage Cultural Landscape that rivals the beauty of Versailles. Located just one hour south of the city, this was originally a hunting lodge, also commissioned in the 16th century by Philip II. It’s a blend of Renaissance and Baroque styles, with lavish rooms filled with tapestries, porcelain, and ornate decorations. There is lots of greenery and gardens here, which is partly why the royal family used this as their spring residence.

Lastly, there’s the historic university city of Alcalá de Henares, the birthplace of Spanish novelist, poet, and playwright Cervantes (he wrote Don Quixote). Just 45 minutes away by car, it also holds its own UNESCO World Heritage status, offering a perfectly preserved look into the Spanish Golden Age (1492–1659).

On your last day, it’s time to get further off the beaten path. Step back into the Golden Age at the Monastery of Las Descalzas Reales and the nearby Royal Monastery of La Encarnación, two hidden palaces that house breathtaking royal art collections behind their cloistered walls.

The Monastery of Las Descalzas Reales is a convent founded in 1559 by Joanna of Austria (sister of Philip II). The name “Descalzas Reales” (which means “Royal Barefoot Ladies”) refers to the Order of Saint Clare nuns who lived here in relative austerity. What makes this monastery unique is that many aristocratic women took refuge here, often bringing their artworks and other valuables. Over the years, this turned the convent into a veritable treasure trove of art.

The Royal Monastery of La Encarnación was founded in 1611 by the wife of Philip III, Margaret of Austria. It was a royal convent, meaning it had the direct support of the monarchy. While the exterior has an austere Baroque style, the interior is quite lavish, with ornate chapels, colorful frescoes, and lots of religious sculptures and paintings. It’s also home to several notable relics, including a vial said to contain the blood of Saint Januarius. While tours are available, it’s still an active convent so parts are not open to the public (which, honestly, makes it all the more interesting to see).

To end your day with a local touch, head to the vast Casa de Campo park. This is the largest public park in the city, created in 1931. It covers a whopping 17 square kilometers (6.5 square miles) and includes an artificial lake, lots of paths, a zoo, and even an amusement park. Here, you can enjoy a scenic lunch or dinner at one of the lakeside terraces, offering an unrivaled view of the Royal Palace and Almudena Cathedral across the skyline. It’s the perfect place to end your trip.

While Barcelona tends to get more attention, I think Madrid is one of the best cities in Europe. It boasts a mouthwatering culinary scene, a raucous nightlife, and tons of things to see and do — no matter your interest or budget. I’ve been visiting the city for almost two decades and never get tired of the tapas, architecture, and history. The city just packs so much in and there really is something for everyone here. By following the itinerary above, you’ll see all the main sights while also getting off the beaten path in Spain’s historic capital.

Despite Barcelona’s famed cuisine, it’s also easy to have a downright disappointing meal if you settle for any old paella-slinging bar along Les Rambles (known in Spanish as La Rambla) or La Barceloneta. (Tip: If a restaurant has to pay someone to stand outside and hassle pedestrians, the food isn’t worth it.)

Start with some classic tapas and paella (both great but neither entirely Catalan) and be sure to seek out local gems like bittersweet vermouth, seasonal seafood, and homestyle Catalan cuisine. But after writing and revising this list for nearly a decade, I have to insist you don’t overlook the city’s burgeoning global cuisines; save room for Calabrese paninis, Northeast Asian sharing plates with natural wines, towering fried chicken sandwiches, South Indian fish stews, and nose-to-tail yakitori.

We update this list quarterly to make sure it reflects the ever-changing Barcelona dining scene. Our write-ups include insider tips from our experienced writers and editors, as well as a rough range of pricing for each destination — ranging from $ for quick, inexpensive meals with dishes largely under $10 (or the equivalent in euros), to $$$$ for places where entrees exceed $30.

Simplicity in concept but perfect execution is what pushes Bemba to the top of the smash burger heap. The menu is brief, with just three burger options: regular cheese, double bacon, and the house special Bemba Burger, which comes with chimichurri and a smashed patty of chorizo criollo sausage in addition to the standard beef patty. Bemba has cultivated a flock of loyal regulars with the conscious selection of quality ingredients, fast service, and careful caramelization of each smashed patty. There’s also a beet and quinoa smash burger, great fries, cold draught beer, and homemade pickles — what more is there to ask for?

Know before you go: It’s small so get there early, or order your burgers for takeaway and eat them in the beautiful Plaça de la Virreina just a few blocks away.

Open for: Lunch and dinner; closed Monday and Tuesday

An offshoot of Mantequerías Pirenaicas, whose truita de patates (Spanish omelet) is famous throughout the city, La Fonda de Pirenaicas is founder Miguel Puchol’s homage to the slow-cooked and indulgent dishes his own grandmother used to prepare; a place to sit and enjoy the flavors of honest Catalan home cooking. With a prime location at the epicenter of the Gràcia neighborhood, “La Fonda” specializes in stews and braises, with a star dish in the macarrones de la iaia Alberta (Grandma Alberta’s macaroni): a gratin of penne pasta tossed in a rich meat ragu and topped with cheese that is melted and caramelized under the searing broiler. Trying at least one of the estofats (stews) is a must — the chickpeas with black sausage and egg, for example — but don’t miss the small plates and tapas, either Here you can find Catalan classics like grilled artichokes with cured egg yolk, bombas, esqueixada de bacalao (a refreshing salad of chilled salt cod pieces with olives, onion, and tomato), and of course, the legendary truita de Pirenaicas.

Know before you go: If you enjoy La Fonda de Pirenaicas, try the affiliated Brasa de Pirenaicas literally next door, specializing in grilled meats.

Barcelona is in the throes of a natural wine renaissance, and Masa Vins is opening some of the most interesting bottles in the city. The cuisine of co-owner chef Antonella Tignanelli is rooted in the Mediterranean but globally inspired. Delicate dishes like seabass crudo with mandarin oil and sumac share space on the menu with bar snacks like the kimchi grilled cheese “bikini.” Brimming with youthful energy, Masa Vins was created by Tignanelli and co-owner Dani Bajc to be a place to hang with friends, drink unique wines, listen to music, and eat excellent food. Of the 250 natural wines on the list, there’s an emphasis on Eastern European labels, and the food menu changes every three months with the seasons.

Know before you go: The place is nearly always busy and there aren’t many tables, so get there early or expect to wait. Groups bigger than five people can book a table between 6 p.m. and 9 p.m., exclusively through Instagram direct message.

From restaurateurs Majid and Mani Alam along with Bilal Khan, Achaar Bar is part of the growing roster of restaurants under the Alam Brothers umbrella. Their latest is an Indian canteen and natural wine bar named for the ubiquitous South Asian pickled vegetable condiment (which they make in-house). The menu spans the breadth of India, as well as adding in some Pakistani favorites like the Lahori samosa, a nod to the Alam brothers’ Punjabi roots. The 150 natural wines on offer have been curated by sommelier Irene Cavalli to pair excellently with the bold seasoning, heat, and aromas of dishes like Kima Mutter Pav (spicy ground beef with peas and brioche bread), spicy coconut prawns, Madrasi vegetables, and Goan fish curry. This kitchen has breathed new life to a space that was once a warehouse, embracing the stripped-down post-industrial aesthetic in Poblenou, a district akin to the Brooklyn of Barcelona. Order as much as you can; the menu isn’t huge, so with a handful of friends you might be able to try it all, and you’ll be happy you did.

Must-try dish: If you’re a meat eater, don’t miss the tandoor-roasted lamb chops, the unassuming star of the menu.

Shoronpo is one of the most popular ramen spots in the city, and it’s equally famous for the eponymous soup dumplings, which are the Japanese version of xiaolongbao. Fillings range from classic minced pork to foie gras, and there is even a dessert shoronpo with chocolate, olive oil, and sea salt. The ramen comes in 10 varieties including three vegetarian options, but the star is the rich and creamy tantanmen with sesame-laced chicken broth, ground pork, and springy noodles that chef Keita Tanaka may have introduced to Barcelona. Booking can be done online and is a must, and keep an eye out for a new location outside the Estació Sants train station soon.

Must-try dish: The karaage (fried chicken) and the yude ebi wantan (boiled shrimp dumplings in sichuan chili sauce) should not be missed.

The team at pizzeria Sartoria Panatieri focuses on seasonality, artisan production, and ingredient provenance, taking the pies to a whole other level and making this one of the hottest spots in the city; in fact, they were awarded the title of 2nd Best Pizza in the World in 2024 as part of the the Best Chef Awards. This self-described “farm to pizza” restaurant meticulously selects every ingredient that graces their creative pizzas. Tender crust from local, organic flour is the base for toppings like sobrasada with Mahón cheese, wild fennel, honey, and mozzarella; adobo pork jowl with celeriac puree, smoked mozzarella, and Brazil nuts; or apple compote with bleu cheese, celery, freeze-dried apple, and caramelized almonds. Make sure you try their house-made salumi as well, especially the papada and the Gascón pork tartare.

Know before you go: The location on Carrer de Provença is the flagship location with the most creative and seasonal pizzas, while the location in Gràcia on Carrer de l’Encarnació serves popular classic pies.

Don’t be misled by the old-school appearance and simple menu at Ultramarinos Marín. Seats at chef Borja García’s open kitchen are among the most sought-after in the city, and for good reason. This humble-looking asador (grill-centric restaurant), only open for breakfast and lunch, is a temple of product-driven Catalan cooking, dedicated to holding classic recipes to the highest standard. Through his training at the iconic Etxebarri and Noma, García works magic with minimal ingredients, a hot plancha, and a smoldering wood-fire oven. The myriad preparations include grilled langoustines, braised pork shanks, mackerel in escabeche, stewed snails, massive steaks, smoked beef tongue, funky garum, and briny sea urchin toast. Don’t miss the fish charcuterie or the simple yet extraordinary grilled smashed potatoes with garlic alioli.

Know before you go: In April 2025, García opened Ultrapaninos Marín next door, a casual sandwich shop and grocery selling some of the house products, from pickles and garums to T-shirts. Don’t miss out on the street-style hot dogs.

Chef Rafa Panatieri, of the Panatieri-Sastre chef duo, loves to say that of all the carefully imagined and artfully presented dishes at Brabo, everyone remembers the bread. Grilled in a pan over glowing coals and served with smoked butter, this unforgettable starter is even better when enjoyed with the salumi that Panatieri and fellow co-owner Jorge Sastre have perfected at their award-winning Sartoria Panatieri pizzería. Most dishes — grilled quail with young garlic, chuletón steak of thickly-marbled Friesian, spring peas with white sobrasada sausage, whole-roasted fish of the day — pass over the grill in Brabo’s kitchen, which is open to the intimate and dimly-lit dining room.

Must-try menu: Opt for the 90 euro tasting menu which includes snacks, appetizers, main dishes, and dessert (the quivering flan with quince caramel should not be missed).

Open for: Dinner on weekdays, with lunch on Friday; closed weekends

Berbena is a leading reference in the blossoming world of chef-driven, modern Catalan cuisine in Barcelona. Take a seat at one of the handful of tables, and begin your meal with hearty chunks of sourdough smeared with rich, smoked butter. Then dive into the assortment of seasonal small plates featuring whatever is fresh that day, from monkfish cheeks with cod tripe pil-pil and beef tongue with bustard, to guinea fowl with parsnips in a Catalan vi ranci sauce and artichokes with truffle; vibrant vegetables often play a starring role in the nightly specials. The cheese selection is impressive, as is their list of the over 600 wines, with more than 30 of them served by the glass. Linger over dessert as you enjoy excellent coffee from nearby SlowMov specialty roasters and espresso from the gleaming La Marzocco machine.

Know before you go: The restaurant has a policy of no children under 12 years old allowed so keep this in mind if traveling or dining with children

Parking Pita is a virtually unmarked restaurant in the corner of what was once a cavernous parking garage. The team uses a wood-fired oven to bake fresh pitas for their popular falafel and shawarma sandwiches. The menu is packed with Mediterranean and Levantine dishes, but also includes forays into North Africa and South Asia — sweet potato and parsnip latkes, Harissa-topped French fries with roasted garlic mayo, beet hummus dotted with pine nuts and feta, grilled leeks with hazelnut dukkah, and roasted cauliflower with curry and pistachios are all great starters. The pitas range from falafel with pickled cabbage and lamb kofta with sumac, to chicken tandoori and charcoal-roasted lamb shawarma. Diners sit at a long communal table and service is quick, making it a popular lunch spot for the neighborhood working crowd.

Know before you go: Parking Pita is just a small part of the Parking Pizza concept —- the excellent and innovative pizzeria at the back of this same, former parking garage. Try their pizzas at several locations around the city, or seek out the massive, grilled ribeye at Parking Sotano, their chic underground steakhouse in Sarrià-Sant Gervasi.

Rooq makes everything in-house, from the herb breading, tangy marinade, fermented pickles, and pillowy brioche buns for their fried chicken sandwiches, to their side dishes and brownies. The menu is minimal: chicken nuggets, salads, and sandwiches. All the chicken sandwiches (fried, spicy fried, grilled, or the new Sichuan-style hotpot flavor) are made with juicy, corn-fed Catalan chicken breasts, and . vegetarians can choose from jackfruit or tofu in either nuggets or sandwiches. Pick your main course and round out the meal with twice-cooked fries tossed in the house spice blend and a side of coleslaw (vinegar- or mayonnaise-based). There’s also a wide selection of craft beers and seven house-made dipping sauces.

Know before you go: On Monday and Tuesday nights, the restaurant has fried chicken wings with special flavors that change weekly.

Seyma Ozkaya Erpul is originally from Turkey but has called Barcelona home for over a decade. She left her previous career as an engineer and marketing professional to pursue her passion for great food and design, opening the first Funky Bakers in the heart of El Born. While that original location remains a popular takeaway coffee shop, the full Funky Bakers experience can be had at the Eatery and Deli location in Eixample. One side is a highly curated gourmet grocery, gift, and coffee shop, while the other is a bistrot serving colorful and creative dishes from around Northern Europe and the Mediterranean, all paired with natural wines.

Know before you go: If you find yourself in the neighborhood of Poble Sec, check out the new Funky location inside the chic Hotel Brumell, with a beautiful interior courtyard tucked away from the busy city center.

Founded in 1898, this family-run colmado has long been one of the most iconic gourmet shops in the city. With an impressive stock of over 200 cheeses and a huge selection of wines, conservas, and cured meats, the famed dry goods store was renovated in 2022 to accommodate an array of high bistro tables, as well as a more formal dining room tucked away behind storeroom doors. The kitchen, led by chef Jordi Vilà of Michelin-starred Alkimia, offers a take on Catalan cuisine that is both elegant and nostalgic, with dishes like wild sea bass tartare with sour cream and caviar, stewed mushrooms with poached egg and zucchini panna cotta, and lamb shoulder braised with Chartreuse.

Know before you go: There are two dining areas, each with its own booking system: the less-formal Colmado Múrria shop space with high tables, and the backroom Rerebotiga with low tables and tablecloths.

Mikan comes up again and again when locals in the know are asked about their favorite spots in the city. Serving what the team calls Northeast Asian cuisine, the project, created together with partners Woody Wang and Arthur Holland Michel, is an evolution of various businesses — private dinners, a lunchbox delivery service, homemade chili crisp — that chef Dan Jin (who goes by Tan) developed at the start of the pandemic. Chef Tan has a particular passion for the dishes of her native region of Northern China, but she also loves the flavors of Japan and Korea. Mikan is a bargain for lunch, when a fixed-price menu includes a variety of dishes, soup, and dessert (including vegan and vegetarian options). In the evenings, the exciting a la carte menu is perfect for sharing. Dishes like grilled koji-cured mackerel with green onion oil, daikon, and shiso, spicy beef salad with cilantro and chile, or Iberian pork loin katsu are paired expertly with an eclectic selection of natural wines, all from small European producers.

Know before you go: One Saturday per month, the bar features rotating local DJs starting at 11 p.m., and there are often collaboration nights with unique, thematic wine pairings by visiting sommeliers, so keep an eye on their Instagram.

The exposed stone walls and traditional Catalan vaulted ceilings of the former water bottling plant that now houses Franca set the scene for what its founders call “invented local cuisine.” The kitchen imagines a parallel universe of Catalan culinary history, delivering dishes inspired by regional classics updated with creative twists. The food is unpretentious yet elegant and modern, forgoing fine-dining clichés like spherifications, heaps of microgreens, and overwrought plating. Instead, the menu focuses on fine-tuned, deeply flavored sharing plates that look deceptively simple, from a custardy Passard egg served in its shell with concentrated garum and local ratafia liqueur, to a salad of ten heirloom Spanish bean varieties, grilled monkfish tail with roasted pepper sauce, and the cuttlefish bikini — a sandwich reminiscent of shrimp toast made with cuttlefish paste pan-fried between crisp bread and served with a smoked pork demi-glace.

Must-order dish: If visiting with a group, order La Festa — a large roasted sharing platter featuring suckling lamb shoulder or pork collar depending on the season, served with seasonal side dishes like tomato and peach salad with charred onions, pork belly skewers, grilled green beans, or roasted potato terrine.

Open for: Dinner, with lunch on Fridays and Saturdays

The Palace hotel, which first opened as the original Ritz Barcelona in 1919, still dazzles with opulent, old-world decor, making it a perfect stage for chef Rafa Zafra’s homage to classic fine dining. Zafra, former head chef at El Bulli, has come to be known for his elegant and simple approach to seafood at his lauded restaurant Estimar, but at Amar the preparations and presentations are decidedly more ornate; there is even a section of dishes inspired by the original Ritz hotel restaurant. The menu also has a section of dishes that pay homage to chef Albert Adrià, as well as top-quality seafood, seven dishes highlighting caviar, a handful of modern Catalan mains, whole grilled fish and steaks, vegetable sides, and Mediterranean tapas.

Must-try menu: There is currently a special menu celebrating the 175th birthday of César Ritz himself, in addition to the standard 12-course tasting menu.

Born in Uruguay and trained in Basque, Spain — both centers for meat in their own right — chef Joaquín Ignacio Sánchez is at the helm of Carnal, a Barcelona boho take on the traditional Spanish steakhouse. At this buzzy spot, hefty cuts of dry-aged beef are charred over oak embers, served medium-rare, and finished at the table on a searing-hot stone slab, allowing diners to dial in their preferred doneness. The steaks on offer come from around the globe and are aged in-house between 45 and 150 days, with prices that range from accessible to wildly extravagant; the menu’s most luxurious item is the 120-day-aged, 2-pound ox chuletón (bone-in rib-eye) from the legendary Bodega El Capricho near León, Spain. Before the main event, warm up with some American black angus beef cheek croquettes, flame-grilled maitake mushrooms, or A5 wagyu tartare kissed with a blowtorch and served over a caveman-esque length of roasted bone marrow. For those who don’t partake in red meat, there is a decent selection of seafood, poultry, and vegetable dishes, providing some welcome balance.

Must-try dish: Do not overlook the “Jamón de vaca Rubia Gallega,” marbled beef from Galicia cured in the style of Iberian ham, which yields intense flavor and fat that dissolves on the tongue.

Chef Rafa Peña’s passion for food is as great as his disregard for formality and fine dining accolades. A favorite among the city’s chefs, Gresca is a must-visit for lovers of nose-to-tail cooking who want to experience the best of seasonal Catalan cuisine without the stuffy service and delicate portions of some Michelin-starred restaurants. The menu can change from day to day, but there are some mainstays that every first-timer at Gresca should experience, such as the roasted chicken with fines herbes, the calf’s brain with butter and lemon, the pan-roasted sweetbreads, and the grilled quail.

Know before you go: The best seats in the house are at the open kitchen pass, where diners get a front row view of the action. It’s worth calling the restaurant to reserve your spot, since they don’t provide this option when booking online.

In an antique bar space with over 100 years of history, on a coveted corner in one of the trendiest parts of the Eixample, Soma opened in 2021 to instant accolades from the gourmands of Barcelona. Cozy decor and a Parisian-style terrace set the stage for a menu of Catalan tapas with a strong Italian character and hints of France. Look for veal liver with onion and red pepper jam, socarrat de risotto (a risotto cooked until compact and crispy like the bottom of a classic paella) with kale and honey, octopus with bacon and paprika potato purée, and ragu-stuffed rigatoni.

Best for: Soma is known as a vibey spot for lunch or dinner, but weekday breakfasts on their tiny outdoor terrace with coffee, pastries, and sandwiches make a perfect, slow morning.

Created by Priscilla Alfaro, a Mexican American woman who grew up in Los Angeles, with her partner Gaston Gabrielli and chef Nick Hosea, this is an excellent, modern American diner where the draw is simple: truly delicious food and bottomless filter coffee from specialty roaster Three Marks Coffee. The pancakes are fluffy, the bacon is crispy, the breakfast burrito is hearty, and the Nashville hot chicken sandwich is tongue-numbingly good. Too many brunch spots in Barcelona rely on Instagram-worthy visuals to compensate for mediocre flavors and cookery, but Gringa All Day is the real deal. Expect a line on the weekends, but the wait is worth it. No reservations.

Know before you go: If you’re a fan of Gringa, check out their burger and fried chicken window at the rock ‘n’ roll/biker-themed cocktail bar Motor Oil BCN.

It’s rare in Barcelona to see a classic trompo slowly, perfectly charring pork, but Xuba Tacos takes tacos al pastor very seriously. The restaurant lovingly layers spiced pork on the spit, shaves it into thin slices, and serves it on supple, handmade blue corn tortillas. Alongside classics like al pastor and carne asada, there are also modern creations, like black beer-batter sea bass tacos with crispy leeks and sauteed lobster tail tacos with cilantro emulsion. Keep an eye out for seasonal specials, like wild mushroom and roasted chestnut tacos, and don’t miss the micheladas.

Best for: For a great deal, opt for their weekday fixed-price lunch menu that includes a starter of guacamole, three tacos of your choice, and a drink (water, soda, or beer).

With no signage or any identifying information visible from the street, the small Suru Bar makes meals in its intimate space feel like serendipity. A row of counter seating gives diners a view into the open kitchen, where chefs prepare star dishes like grilled chicken skin skewers with chilled shrimp tartare, vibrant spring peas with smoked chorizo and cured egg yolks, and a brochette of crunchy pig ear with charred chicken gizzard and tender squid. The cuisine is seasonal, thanks to the prime location just across the street from the famous Ninot market, and the dishes range from yakitori on a traditional Japanese grill to explorations of all things offal, with an extensive wine list to round out the meal.

Must-try dish: Don’t miss the grilled veal sweetbreads with a silky puree of toasted cauliflower and pickles.

Crowds flock to Taktika Berri to down copious amounts of ice-cold beer and funky, sour cider while perusing the platters of pintxos that adorn the well-polished bar. Highly traditional without being kitsch, this popular Basque bar, founded in 1995, feels like it’s been transported straight from the industrial port city of Bilbao, complete with old photos and jai lai baskets. The bar area at the entrance can get packed with an after-work crowd, who graze classic pintxos like fried chistorra sausage, anchovies with piquillo peppers, and tortilla de bacalao (salt cod omelette). But don’t be deceived; the pintxos on display are just a small fraction of what the menu has to offer. Take a look at the carta and order from a selection of Basque mainstays like almejas con refrito (sauteed clams with garlic, cayenne, and cider vinegar), the two-person serving of cogote de merluza (a large center-cut piece of whole hake, roasted and served with garlic and parsley), fried monkfish loin, or a well-marbled txultetón steak.

Know before you go: In addition to the cold or room-temperature pintxos on the bar, there is also a menu of hot pintxos that can be made-to-order (try the morcilla black sausage). To drink, choose a caña (small draught beer), Basque hard cider, or Txakoli (a famous, lightly-sparkling, kind of saline, low-alcohol wine from the Basque region).

True to its name, eggs take center stage at this relaxed restaurant known for spins on the classic Spanish potato and egg omelet (truita de patates). In the quiet, upscale neighborhood of Les Corts, Manda Huevos combines the relaxed energy of a trendy vermutería with sharp, modern branding. Chef and co-founder Ot Salvans draws on his experience in some of Spain’s top fine-dining kitchens while bringing flavor influences from around the globe. Catalan mainstays like cap i pota veal stew, langoustine-stuffed roast chicken, and macarrons del nét (baked pasta with meat ragù topped with a broiled cheese gratin) share the menu with fusion creations like roasted bone marrow with scallop tartare, fermented lemon, and caviar, and the chili crab-stuffed eggs. The nine truita varieties on the menu range from guanciale carbonara and Menorcan sobrasada with Mahón cheese, to red shrimp tartar with shrimp jus and the signature “bomba” omelet filled with pork ragù, spicy brava sauce, and saffron alioli. Omelets come in three flexible sizes, with the exception of the open-faced garlic lobster omelet, which skips potatoes entirely and comes topped with sherry-confited piquillo peppers and lobster roe.

Know before you go: Wednesday through Friday at lunch, take advantage of the fixed-price four-course meal. It includes an aperitivo, first course, second course, dessert or coffee, and a drink, showcasing many of the menu’s best-sellers.

With Taberna Nardi, founder Martín Pimentel of the Grup Amicks restaurant group has traded the vibrant red color scheme and meat-heavy menu of his popular Bar Pimentel for a calming vibe of deep maritime blue just a few doors down. Nardi is a self-proclaimed “irreverent seafood tavern” with a shimmering wave-like mirrored ceiling, just a handful of tables, and an inviting bar with open kitchen where the day’s catch is displayed on ice. Try the cumin-spiked hake pincho moruno skewers off the smoky Japanese robata, the grilled zamburiña scallops with tomato confit and garlic, the delicate cured horse mackerel with ginger and jalapeño, or the surprisingly meaty braised tuna cheek with potato purée. Wash it all down with a saline-heavy white wine, a natural sparkling, or their famous oyster-infused dry martini.

Must-try dish: While it may not be the immediate choice for most diners, the rich, buttery cube of toasted brioche topped with a dollop of tangy crème fraîche and a succulent cured anchovy is a single bite that should not be missed. And don’t forget the cheesecake for dessert.

Since 1946, Gelida has been an important social hub for the neighborhood, be it old friends meeting for a weekend lunch or workers loading up on calories before sunrise. The menu is full of classic Catalan dishes, but the breakfast-forward spot is best known for esmorzars de forquilla, or “fork breakfasts.” In modern Barcelona, many people opt for a light breakfast (think croissant and coffee), but the tradition of beginning your day with a hearty plate of stewed veal, tripe, snails, pig trotter, chickpeas, or pork cheeks is still alive at institutions like Gelida. You’ll see heaps of fried potatoes topped with a runny fried egg and spicy chorizo on tables as life-long regulars drink cafe con leche and mini draught beers simultaneously.

Must-try dish: Cap i pota, a gelatin-rich stew of veal head and hoof (though Gelida uses veal necks, which are meatier). The sauce is meant to be sopped up with pieces of crusty baguette and washed down with a house Gandesa, a golden, long-macerated white wine from the region of Terra Alta.

With a wave of modern kebab shops hitting Barcelona over the last year, sometimes it feels good to go back to an all-time favorite. Carrer de Joaquin Costa is always buzzing with activity, as is its most famous kebab shop, Bismillah Kebabish, a halal mainstay in the Pakistani community of Barcelona since 2003. Crowds pack into the minimalist dining room, often sharing tables with strangers, just for a taste of the famous and affordable chicken shawarma — fresh naan that is pulled by hook from a tandoor oven and loaded with fragrant, bright-red shavings of spiced chicken thigh, carved from the spinning skewer that the team prepares daily (it’s much better than the beef version). Mixed with roasted peppers and onions and topped with your choice of fresh vegetables, chile sauce, and garlic sauce, this is the go-to meal from many regular customers. However, dishes such as the lamb curry, beef shish kebab, and the tandoori chicken skewers are also quite popular; you can even mix and match multiple dishes together on the combo plates with rice and salad.

Know before you go: This is one of the last Barcelona institutions that is still cash-only, so come prepared.

La Mudana is celebrating its 10th anniversary in 2025, and over the last decade it has become an anchor of the local culinary scene of Sants, a neighborhood known for its tight-knit community and support for local businesses. La Mudana is equally suited as a spot for friends to catch up over a few tapas and a beer as it is for a refined multicourse dinner. The food draws influence as much from Mediterranean and Catalan cuisine as it does from the flavors of France and Asia, with a little Latin American influence as well. Pick from dishes like the kimchi juice and comté cheese oyster, meaty cap i pota rice with teriyaki eel, ox tail-stuffed brioche with smoked sabayon, and the Iberian pork katsu sando with mole and ají amarillo, or opt for a fixed-price two-person tasting menu of six all-star dishes, plus dessert, that includes vermouth to start and cava to finish.

Must-try dish: No table at La Mundana is complete without the picturesque patatas bravas — two perfectly-sculpted rectangles of potato, fried crisp and topped with classic spicy brava sauce as well as the funky black garlic alioli.

This small corner bar just one block from the old port of Barcelona has been serving the same four dishes since 1945. Though the salted anchovies, fried sausage, and tomato salad are all delicious, pescaditos (fresh, floured, and fried whole anchovies) are the true star. Now run by the grandson of the original founder, Bar La Plata sells over 85 pounds of the little fishes per week.

Know before you go:The place is nearly always busy and only has a few tables, so either show up right when they open, or do as the local crowd does and eat standing up at the bar or even in the street if no seats are available. Drinking alcohol outside isn’t allowed but you can get the pescaditos to-go in a paper cone.

Plump pork meatballs braised in tomato sauce, creamy stracciatella cheese, grilled zucchini, and a dusting of grated ricotta salata come together in one of the newest sandwiches on the menu at Compà, a panini shop dedicated to the flavors of Calabria. In addition to the original location a few streets over from the Barceloneta beach, owner Vittorio Cicero has recently expanded with a new shop in the uptown neighborhood of Gràcia, with panini like mortadella with burrata and pistachio pesto or Calabrese lardo with spicy ‘nduja spread, smoked provolone, and roasted potatoes. Choose from nearly a dozen sandwiches including rotating specials, all served on 48-hour slow-fermented focaccia, pressed and piping hot.

Know before you go: There are a few tables, but the sandwiches are meant as street food, so get yours to-go and head to the beach or the nearest plaza. Just don’t forget the napkins, and a tiramisu for dessert.

One of the oldest restaurants in the beachside neighborhood of La Barceloneta, this is the birthplace of the famous “bomba de la Barceloneta,” a mouth­watering fried potato croquette stuffed with savory ground beef and topped with aioli and hot sauce, which now appears on menus all over the city. In addition to the bombas, try the calamars a la planxa (grilled squid), and in summer get the grilled sardines. Suculent isn’t defined by one genre of cuisine, but packs its tasting and a la carte menus with as many delicious dishes as possible. Dishes change with the seasons, but don’t miss mainstays such as the beets with beurre blanc and smoked eel, the royal custard of porcini mushrooms with sea urchin, and the braised hare canelon with foie gras and Codium seaweed.

Now with three locations across the city, Lluritu’s stripped-down approach to a Mediterranean marisquería has made it a must-visit in the Barcelona food scene. Its ethos of shunning complex sauces, tablecloths, and deep fryers in favor of simple, wood-fired and planxa grilling means diners get the pure flavors of the region’s best seasonal seafood.
