Mexico City studio Manifiesto calls the project "Mexicute is a mood," and the phrase carries the whole concept: Peko-Peko starts from a pair of charming mascots and grows into what the studio describes as a boundless universe, blending the timeless charm of Japanese culture with the vibrancy of Mexican expression. The anchor is the axolotl, Mexico's unmistakable salamander, reimagined as a pink 3D character with a flower crown and a tiny cross-body bag. Around it the studio builds a cast, a cat and a frog, each ready to melt into caps, packaging, and print. The palette does as much work as the characters: bubblegum pink, grape purple, mint, peach, and lime, applied in soft 3D scenes with the kind of squishy lighting that makes every render feel touchable. Nothing here is decorated for decoration's sake; the joy is the system.

The other wears pink glitter with a purple outline and a white cloud halo on peach. The letters read like something you could squeeze. Supporting type stays quiet, a clean grotesque for names and phone numbers on the stationery, so the balloon letters keep all the volume.

Every artifact plays along. The characters become bottle caps: a cat lid on an amber bottle, a frog on a yellow one, the axolotl on purple. A strawberry-scented body wash arrives as a pink bear-shaped bottle wearing the wordmark on its belly. The business card is die-cut with a scalloped edge that quotes the balloon letters. The letterhead keeps the pink wordmark small and centered on white, framed by a single star. It is a world, not a logo sheet.

#74. Brighton are the apex B club – their next target should be a Champions League place | Jonathan Wilson

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Diego Gómez’s wondergoal in a five-star demolition is just the latest in an impressive run of results for the south coast team

For two or three years now, it’s been standard to nod approvingly at how well-run Brighton, Brentford and Bournemouth are, and to move on. What else is there to say? It’s true, of course – these are sides who have made the most of relatively limited resources. But there’s also an underlying assumption in the designation: that these are clubs who have no higher aspiration, that their limit is earning praise for upper mid-table finishes.

This season, perhaps, offers an opportunity to change that. Other than Arsenal, every major club is undergoing significant transition. Manchester City may have started the season with seven straight wins, but there’s every chance the punishment for their multiple breaches of Premier League regulations takes them out of the race for the top five. In that context, why shouldn’t one of those efficient smaller sides qualify for the Champions League?

Brentford began the weekend in third and with, perhaps, a slight sense of frustration that they had drawn four games. Again, they will feel they might have done rather better on Saturday; they were leading Liverpool 1-0 when Yehor Yarmolyuk foolishly flicked his head towards Jérémy Jacquet and was sent off just before half-time. The result was a 2-1 defeat and a sense of an opportunity missed. Bournemouth have not had the easiest start under Marco Rose, but Sunday’s 2-0 win over Sunderland perhaps hints at a brighter future.

Which left Brighton to carry the charge of the B clubs, which they did in emphatic style, ending a run of four derbies without success with a crushing 5-0 win. Crystal Palace had been extremely fortunate to survive a first-half battering from Nottingham Forest last week. They were thoroughly outplayed in the early stages again, and this time found themselves 3-0 down inside 10 minutes. Perhaps the opening goal, a cross headed into his own net by Jaydee Canvot, was fortunate, but it was an error induced by the intensity of Brighton’s start. The second goal, a perfectly weighted drive from just inside the Palace half from Diego Gómez, spoke of a side playing with immense confidence. Already this season, 11 different players have scored for Brighton in the league. They have become the image of a side without stars, the whole far greater than the individuals who comprise it.

The difference from February, when Brighton last played Palace, is stark. They lost that game 1-0, meaning they had won just one of their previous 12 Premier League games. Generalised grumbling had begun to curdle into calls for Fabian Hürzeler to be sacked. When they lost Carlos Baleba, Jan Paul van Hecke, Danny Welbeck and James Milner over the summer, there were reasons to wonder whether they could keep going with the process of continuous renewal.

There were the usual assorted signings, the usual mix of Premier League experience and exotic unknowns. The 19-year-old centre-back Luka Vušković, who spent last season on loan at Hamburg from Tottenham, already looks at home (and who really thought, when Spurs and Brighton effectively swapped centre-backs, with Van Hecke moving to London, that Brighton would not end up with the better of the deal?). The 21-year-old Spanish midfielder Chema Andrés, who arrived from Stuttgart, sparkled from the bench against Arsenal and has excelled in the two games since.

But the stand-out performer this season has been a far more familiar name: Pascal Gross, who returned in January after a season and a half at Borussia Dortmund. No player in the Premier League has registered more than his four assists this season. Nobody has run further. He has three goals. He averages a league-high 3.0 key passes a game. To bring back a player who is now 35 has been a masterstroke.

The victory over Arsenal before the international break suggested a team capable not merely of accumulating points against the league’s lesser lights, or even of challenging the elite, but of overwhelming them. Arsenal were suffocated by the Brighton press and, while a couple of low-xG long-range finishes in the first half helped, the fact is that Brighton dominated the match to a remarkable extent.

The question is whether they can keep it going. Just as their transfer policy is now an accepted feature of the Premier League, so too is Brighton’s mid-season slump. They haven’t won a game in December since 2023. Is this squad strong enough to sustain the challenge? Europe seems not to be unsettling Brighton as it did on their first foray, or as it appears to be draining Bournemouth, Crystal Palace and Sunderland – which may be a reflection on the relative quality of the Conference League.

But if any of those B clubs are ever going to break into the Champions League, they may never have a better opportunity than Brighton do this season.

#65. James McClean: ‘Wearing the poppy was just never going to happen’

📅 Oct 17, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The former Republic of Ireland international on growing up in a community still reeling from the Troubles and the controversy that scarred his career

“The perception of me is that, basically, I am a madman,” James McClean says calmly. “I’m outspoken. I’m a loudmouth. I’m very opinionated and I hate all things British. That’s from people who don’t know me. The reality is quite the opposite. I’m pretty boring. I go training, go home and switch off. A lot of my days are quite bland, quite basic.”

McClean sounds as sane as he looks gaunt on a bleak Monday in Derry. He spent 14 years in English football and the fact that he is now at war with his own club, Derry City, can’t obscure the fact that he is the fifth-most-capped Republic of Ireland international, having played 103 times. “People define success in different ways,” he says, “but I’m the only footballer from the north of Ireland to win over 100 caps for Ireland. I played 150 Premier League games over five seasons, 800 professional games and won a lot of individual accolades. I count that as success and don’t look back with regrets.”

But there’s sadness, too, in the way his life and career were scarred by his refusal to wear the poppy when playing games around Remembrance Sunday in England. “It’s completely changed me as a person,” McClean says. “It’s made me a bit of a recluse because I don’t trust a lot of people now. I’ve been stung bad.”

McClean’s autobiography, The Walls, is one of the best sports books of the year. It’s a powerful and sometimes disturbing read elevated by McClean’s willingness to show himself in an unflattering light.

I have written a lot about Derry and boxing, and Bloody Sunday, but it helps to be back here to understand how McClean can respect the millions of soldiers who died in two world wars while refusing to forget how the British army killed so many Irish people in this city.

He comes from Creggan, the estate affected most on Bloody Sunday in 1972 when 14 unarmed civilians were killed by the British army in Derry. Six of the fatalities were from Creggan. In 2010, after the Saville report finally concluded that all the victims posed no threat, the British prime minister David Cameron apologised for the “unjustified and unjustifiable” massacre.

McClean was born in Derry in 1989 and he writes that “learning how to make a petrol bomb was one of my first life lessons. I was 11 before I’d properly mastered it.”

He pauses when asked how he would feel if any of his three daughters or his son, who is 11, ended up making petrol bombs. “I’m very thankful they don’t have to. It’s a different era now. I just want them to be proud to be Irish.”

The 37-year-old spells out the contrast with his youth. “I grew up in a very Republican community still dealing with the effects of the Troubles. You would see gunmen on the street. People got shot, kneecapped. To get access to my street you had to go through a lane where you sometimes saw masked men doing punishment beatings. But you weren’t scared. You knew you’d done nothing wrong. That was the norm … but it’s not normal.”

His biological father, Joe Kelly, was an alcoholic in Creggan and McClean spoke to him only once – when he shouted at him in the street to stay away. But he remembers a happy childhood and, when his professional career began with Derry City, “I was very carefree”.

McClean was 22 when Steve Bruce signed him for Sunderland in August 2011. He made his Premier League debut that December after Bruce was replaced by Martin O’Neill. McClean soon became a regular starter and that first season in English football was full of joy and praise. He was fast, skilful and disciplined. Apart from wasting money his biggest vice was typing his name on Twitter where he would revel in glowing reports of his play.

“It was amazing,” McClean says. “I never thought about the politics when I first got to England so up until November 2012, when the poppy thing started, I never experienced any bother.”

Everything changed when he told Sunderland he wouldn’t wear the poppy because it also represented the British army which had occupied Derry for so long. “There was never a decision for me to make,” he says. “Wearing the poppy was just never going to happen. It was a very difficult year and I felt a lot of that could have been handled better by the club. But Sunderland came out with a statement before the game which drew all eyes on me. It was there to protect the club. They basically said this is James’s personal decision but we, as a club, wholeheartedly support the poppy appeal.

“I wanted to make my own statement but I was constantly told by the club that: ‘No, no, it’ll die over. Just leave it.’ I’d just turned 23 and I thought the club had a duty of care to look after me and that’s what they were doing. I’d won the player of the year the previous season but I felt they hung me out to dry.”

McClean recalls that: “The next game was away to Fulham on a Sunday, on Sky. A threat was made that I would be shot if I came on to the pitch. I remember [his wife] Erin panicking. It sounds extreme and people might say: ‘Oh, wise up, you’re not going to be shot on TV.’ But the night before the game I was assigned two police officers to stand outside my door. So the Met police took it seriously and that made it more real.”

O’Neill brought him on in the second half and his every touch was booed – by the Fulham and Sunderland supporters. The vitriol poisoned his world but McClean kept searching for his name on Twitter. He became furious whenever, after a mutually respectful encounter with a member of the public, the person would go online to abuse him.

Sometimes McClean would track the person down and demand an apology. “Maybe that madness was always there, but it got stronger,” he notes.

McClean moved to Wigan before joining West Brom where, in “the best kind of madness” he earned £42,000 a week while he had his best spell in the Premier League. But as one poppy season blurred into another, the death threats kept coming, as did the warnings that Erin would be raped. McClean says: “My reaction was always to fight fire with fire. I went into some games in the wrong frame of mind. It wasn’t: ‘Can I help the team?’ It was: ‘I hope I score today so I can shush them up.’”

McClean admits: “There were days where I went looking for the conflict.” He posted a photograph of himself listening to his favourite Wolfe Tones song, The Broad Black Brimmer. “I knew exactly what I was doing,” McClean writes. “It was an IRA song – and I was sure it would rile a certain type of person up.”

By the time he reached Stoke, for whom he played 111 games between 2018 and 2021, his problems in English football reached a new low. “Brexit made the idiots louder” and “the poppy season seemed to be getting longer every year”. He had also made a stupid mistake during lockdown when he posted a photograph of himself, wearing a balaclava, with his two eldest children below a caption which read: ‘Today’s school lesson – History.’”

McClean thought he was being funny but his choice not to wear the poppy, in contrast, was heartfelt. “I thought others might have followed suit but it never turned out to be the case.”

Did he feel that a more rounded understating of his stance had emerged by the time he enjoyed his happiest seasons in English football, while playing for a Hollywood-tinged Wrexham from 2023 to earlier this year? “A wee bit, aye. When you explain it to footballers you’ve played with, and to people on a personal level, it opens their eyes and they say: ‘Oh, I understand that.’ But the overall British population? I don’t think so.”

Reflecting on his Premier League years, McClean marvels at the brilliance of Kevin De Bruyne and Eden Hazard. “You had to accept there were lads capable of doing things that were beyond you,” McClean writes. “But there were also games where I thought I was coming up against a physical strength that I found suspicious. Coming back into the Premier League after a break convinced me that some players had to be benefiting from some kind of performance-enhancing support.

“I remember coming up against one player that I’d encountered at Sunderland who I always felt that I’d been able to match in terms of his speed and power. While I’d been away from the Premier League, he’d gone to a bigger club, and now he was a different animal completely.”

These are startling allegations and, while McClean has no concrete evidence to support his doubts, he trusts his gut reaction. “I’ve always been very fanatical about my fitness,” he says. “But when you come against the top Premier League clubs, no matter what height, what size, they’re just faster, they’re stronger, and you’re thinking: ‘How is that possible?’”

After their daughter Willow was diagnosed with global development delay and autism, Erin persuaded him to be tested for autism. He was found to be “at the high-functioning end of the spectrum” but he still struggles with the fact that “I’ve probably got body dysmorphia or some kind of bulimia. Maybe it’s the autism thing, because I’m so strict with my diet 95% of the time. But when I can’t work out I binge. So if I’m travelling and flying, I’ll just eat that much shite to the point where I have to make myself sick. I still feel really guilty, because the next day I’ll punish myself in the gym – which is not healthy.”

Life has become even more complicated since he returned to Derry City in January. “I was meant to be coming home for the fairytale ending. But you quickly realise the promises are just bullshit.”

McClean has not played for the club since July and, asked about the situation, he says: “It’s not good. It’s a player against owner and there’s only one winner. I think that’s it in terms of Derry City. You never think you’ll be betrayed by your own so it’s painful.”

When contacted by the Guardian, a spokesman for Derry City said: “The club cannot speak to, or comment on, how James feels.” He also rejected the suggestion that Derry City had failed to fulfil any promises made to McClean.

Earlier this month, McClean said he couldn’t “understand or accept” the way he was being treated by the club and that he was fit to play despite being left out of the squad by interim manager Mark Connolly. Derry City then released a statement in which they said: “The club has sought to resolve this internal matter privately. Out of respect for confidentiality and the fact that this dispute involves sensitive medical details regarding James’ hip condition, the club have previously refrained from making any public statement on the matter.”

McClean insists he could play football elsewhere but how does he feel if this is the end of his career? “It doesn’t unsettle me because I’ve been boxing off and on for 10 years and in the past 18 months I’ve really thrown myself into it. I’m not going to start a pro boxing career because it’s just not feasible at my age. But I’ve been in contact with Misfits and they want me to fight.”

#273. IEEE Offers More Services to Cities That Want to Be Smarter

More and more city governments are using advanced technologies to improve livability for their residents. Municipalities use data from smartphones and sensors for traffic enforcement, traffic control, and waste management. They also have implemented artificial intelligence and deep learning programs.

Dubai, Paris, and Coral Gables, Fla., are among the cities that have invested heavily in programs to become smarter.

But along with the conveniences that come with a smart city transition, there have been challenges including safety and privacy issues and eroding relationships between residents and city officials.

Although many initiatives have delivered real innovations, not all have been successful. Inefficient global coordination has hindered projects with unclear standards, socioeconomic differences, and a lack of worldwide governance, leading to a disconnect between the technologies and the ability to adapt to them.

Citizens’ trust in the technologies suffers because of privacy breaches, personal data leaks, and other issues.

Finding solutions

Smart city projects are complex and expensive endeavors that involve politicians, citizens, technocrats, and engineers. Successful projects require citizens to be fully engaged and to have strong collaborations with the participants, especially engineers, as engineering is the cornerstone.

Collaboration with universities, research institutions, and technology experts can bridge knowledge gaps on the behavior of complex socio-technical systems. What’s missing, though, is a global platform where those involved can discuss their experiences.

To help solve those and other challenges, the IEEE Smart Cities Technical Community (formerly the IEEE Smart Cities Initiative) was launched in 2013 by IEEE Future Directions, which incubates emerging technologies and new applications of current ones. The technical community provides city leaders and others with credible, unbiased information and educational content developed by IEEE experts.

Since then, IEEE Smart Cities has launched services including its flagship International Smart Cities Conference, a media center with educational and technical resources, and a contest to recognize the best smart project completed by city officials. The platforms bring together practicing engineers, academic researchers, policymakers, and city leaders to collaborate and exchange ideas and experiences about moving toward a smart-city model.

Better serving humanity

In 2021 the IEEE Power & Energy Society began managing the Smart Cities program, which is supported by the IEEE Communications, Industry Applications, and Intelligent Transportation Systems societies and other IEEE entities.

Thanks to volunteers’ dedication and determination, IEEE Smart Cities launched several services. But the initiative’s structure limited the development of long-term projects and the creation of new activities.

To address the situation, the IEEE Technical Activities Board (TAB) created the second-generation technical community ad hoc committee, also in 2021. It was a pilot program whereby IEEE Smart Cities formed a board of governors to oversee the community’s operations and leadership, and created a policies and procedures manual.

In November 2025 IEEE Smart Cities became a TAB technical community.

IEEE Smart Cities now

Today more than 7,000 volunteers collaborate on various aspects of the community within at least one of the following technical tracks:

  • Smart grid.
  • Smart water management.
  • Green electronics for smart cities.
  • Smart governance and planning.
  • Hydrogen for smart cities.
  • Smart and sustainable campus.
  • Security for smart cities.
  • Smart lighting for cities.
  • Smart mobility for cities.
  • Artificial intelligence for cities.

New webinar series, workshops, conferences, educational resources, and other offerings are being created.

Two events were launched recently: the IEEE European Technology and Engineering Management Summit, held in May 2025 in Belgium, with the next one planned for 2027; and the IEEE Sustainable Smart Lighting World Conference, scheduled for 2 to 6 December in Athens.

The Smart Cities Ambassadors program aims to raise the technical community’s visibility outside IEEE by holding roundtables and other events at non-IEEE functions.

IEEE Smart Cities volunteers are working on launching journals. In addition, standards are being developed in collaboration with the IEEE Standards Association, societies, and councils.

The program is collaborating with the Open and Agile Smart Cities and Communities network, the Instituto de Competitividad e Innovación para el Desarrollo y Transferencia de Tecnología, and the International Project Management Association.

All of those actions have the ultimate objective of boosting IEEE Smart Cities’ growth and sustainability, as well as strengthening the leadership and educational offerings to technical community volunteers.

#382. Buy The Finished Home, Skip The Remodeling Misery

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

At almost 50, I’m never doing a gut remodel again.

Everyone who has survived one has the same thousand-yard stare. It’s the look of someone who spent two years arguing about grout color and waiting on inspectors who never show up when they say they will.

Remodeling might be the most unpleasant thing you can voluntarily sign up for. Nobody makes you do it. Yet every year, homeowners line up to hand over hundreds of thousands of dollars for the privilege of being miserable.

Cost overruns are common. So are time overruns.

If you’re brave enough to build a house or do a gut remodel, budget for the project to cost at least 50% more and take 50% longer than expected. If you do, you might come out only slightly bloodied and bruised. If you don’t, and you trust your general contractor’s estimate, your silent stress will go through the roof and detonate every relationship around you.

Here’s a great chart compiled by ResiClub from a report by University of Chicago and Princeton professors titled “Where Is It Slow To Build, And Is It Getting Slower?”

I’m not shocked to see San Francisco at #1, averaging 1.1 years to get a permit and 3.8 years to build a single-family home. San Francisco, after all, is the most expensive city to raise a family.

Miami and Philadelphia are close behind at about 3.4 years, while Indianapolis, El Paso, and Colorado Springs get it done in roughly six months. The national average from permit application to completion is about 8.8 months.

So a San Franciscan could watch a family in Indianapolis build seven homes in the time it takes to finish one. And it’s getting worse. Nationally, single-family permitting time roughly tripled from 1.6 months in 2000 to 4.9 months in 2023.

I’ve gone through two gut remodels and a couple of bathroom remodels since first buying San Francisco property in 2003, and every one has been a hellish process.

My last gut remodel started in mid-2019 and wasn’t 100% done until the end of 2022. Granted, COVID delayed completion by about six months. But three years for a project I thought would take one year at most was brutal.

At least I was able to run out the upstairs portion of the house while the ground floor gut remodel languished.

Here’s what most buyers miss. The construction bill is only the visible part of the iceberg.

Let’s say you buy a $1.6 million fixer and budget $400,000 for a gut remodel over 18 months. Reasonable, right?

Now apply reality. The budget runs 50% over, so it’s $600,000. The timeline stretches to 2.5 years. Meanwhile, you need somewhere to live. At $6,500 a month in rent, that’s $195,000. You’re also paying roughly 1.2% in property tax on a house you can’t live in, about $48,000 over 2.5 years.

Add it up and your $1.6 million fixer actually cost you about $2.44 million. And I haven’t even counted the opportunity cost of tying up roughly $850,000 in cash that could have been compounding in stocks or private investments.

Then there’s the cost you can’t put on a spreadsheet. If your kids are five and seven when you start, they’re eight and ten by the time you move in. You don’t get those years back living out of boxes in a rental while texting your GC at 10 pm.

If you’re over 40 and plan to buy in a city where building takes longer than 1-1.5 years, I highly encourage you to buy a fully remodeled home. You’ll save so much time, money, and headache.

At least in San Francisco, the market undervalues fully remodeled homes. Given how painful remodeling is, they should trade at much bigger premiums than they do. I suspect the same is true in most slow-to-build cities. Take a look at this illustrative chart.

Today, the savings have shrunk to about $60,000. Labor costs, material costs, and permitting delays keep eating away at the discount, while finished homes haven’t gotten much pricier relative to fixers.

Spread $60,000 over 2.5 years of construction and you’re earning about $2,000 a month to manage contractors, chase inspectors, and live in a rental. And that assumes nothing else goes wrong. One bad subcontractor or a three-month permit delay and the savings disappear entirely.

#276. Chasing China’s Monazite Monopoly

📅 Oct 15, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →



One of the singular features of Baotou, an industrial city of 2 million people in Inner Mongolia, is a 10-square-kilometer (4-square-mile) lake that is among the most polluted in the world. The artificial lake, referred to by the name of the dam associated with it, Weikuang Dam (or Baogang Tailings Dam), has for decades collected the effluent from minerals processing for China’s globally dominant iron, steel, and rare-earths industries.

Built in the late 1950s, the lake contains a highly toxic stew of industrial chemicals with substantial amounts of radioactive thorium, the latter resulting primarily from the processing of monazite ores for rare-earths. China has since greatly tightened its environmental regulations and restrictions on mining and minerals processing. For example, largely because of the environmental disaster that is Weikuang Dam, the country doesn’t permit the development or operation of stand-alone monazite mines—that is, mines where nothing is mined except monazite ore.

Now, with industrialized nations in North America, Asia, and Europe spending billions to shore up their rare-earths supply chains, and counting on monazite to help them do it, China’s history and experiences with monazite offer important lessons.

This article is a collaboration between Foreign Policy, the global magazine of politics and ideas, and IEEE Spectrum, the flagship magazine of the IEEE.

Monazite ore is a pillar of China’s rare-earths industry because it is a prime source of some less common but extremely important rare-earth elements, notably dysprosium, terbium, yttrium, and erbium. The country mines and processes vast quantities of monazite ore; it also imports huge amounts of monazite ore for processing.

Most of the domestically mined monazite ore comes from a single mine: the mammoth Bayan Obo complex, 150 kilometers north of Baotou, in Inner Mongolia. Bayan Obo, which is primarily an iron-ore mine, also happens to contain the largest known rare-earth deposit in the world. It’s the source of nearly half of all the rare-earth ore mined on the planet in a typical year. Most of the rare-earth ore mined at Bayan Obo is a type called bastnaesite; it’s the mining of that bastnaesite that makes it legal to mine monazite ore at Bayan Obo as well.

Related: Can a Radioactive Ore Break China’s Grip on Rare Earths?

All told, ore from Bayan Obo was used to produce 170,000 tonnes of rare-earth metal last year, according to David Merriman, research director at the minerals intelligence firm Project Blue. Roughly 30 percent to 40 percent of the rare-earth ore mined at Bayan Obo is monazite. That amount alone would be enough to make China the world’s largest processor of monazite for rare-earths.

China’s 2025 Monazite Imports Topped 70,000 Tonnes

But a more detailed picture of China’s interest in monazite emerges when you consider the country’s imports of the ore. Last year, they amounted to more than 70,000 tonnes, says Thomas Kruemmer, a Singapore-based consultant on minerals and metals. That figure becomes more impressive when you consider the unique challenges of shipping monazite by sea. Because of its radioactivity, concentrated monazite ore is classified as Class 7 dangerous cargo under the International Maritime Dangerous Goods Code. That means, Kruemmer notes, that the ore can be imported through only four ports in China.

In addition, importation of some kinds of monazite into China is supposed to go through a joint venture consisting of the China National Nuclear Corp. and Shenghe Resources, which are at least partly state-owned. But lots of imported monazite doesn’t go through those organizations, notes Merriman. That’s because heavy mineral sands, which may contain roughly 2 percent or 3 percent monazite by weight, are not subject to the same import controls as concentrated monazite ore. So companies import the heavy mineral sands; separate out various profitable concentrates, including the monazite mineral concentrate; and then process the monazite concentrate to recover the rare-earths.

China has lately stepped up its efforts to secure rare-earth ore around the world. One reason, says Kruemmer, is simply to make up for recently lost supplies. In 2025, the U.S. government invested US $400 million in MP Materials, which operates the Mountain Pass mine and processing facility in California. The deal put an end to long-standing contracts under which MP Materials had been selling tens of thousands of tonnes of rare-earth concentrate annually to a trading affiliate of Shenghe Resources.

But other Westerners speculate that China has strategic reasons for stepping up its efforts to acquire heavy mineral sands or other rare-earth assets abroad. By locking down rights to deposits or projects abroad, Chinese organizations are making them unavailable to non-Chinese developers, according to this reasoning.

Merriman points out that your view of these activities largely depends on where you sit. The U.S. government recently made a conditional loan commitment of up to $725 million to U.S. monazite processor Energy Fuels, which has been aggressively securing substantial monazite supplies in Australia, Brazil, and Madagascar. To a Chinese observer, those moves probably look rather strategic.

China, meanwhile, is doing what it has been doing for decades. “China’s playing its game,” says Merriman.
