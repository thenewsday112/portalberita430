#301. Castlevania: Belmont&#039;s Curse Review - Worthy Of The Whip

📅 Oct 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Castlevania has been a dormant franchise for years, but Belmont's Curse revives the series with fiery passion. There are hundreds of indies inspired by Konami's gothic vampire-hunting games, putting a lot of pressure on the publisher to live up to its far-reaching legacy. Konami's surprising, yet effective solution is to bring in a new developer: Evil Empire, best known for its work on the Castlevania-inspired Dead Cells. The resulting game has a fresh perspective, a brand-new protagonist, and whip-smart execution.

Rose Belmont and her father Trevor have been summoned to Paris, a city under supernatural siege. Rose is eager to prove her worth, partially to impress her father, who killed Dracula 23 years ago, and partially because she wants revenge for her bewitched arm, a curse from Dracula, passed down from her late mother. It's a solid premise, and while the narrative is not particularly complex, Rose's character arc has standout moments that will stick with me.

For newcomers, Belmont's Curse stands alone perfectly fine, though it is technically a sequel to Castlevania III, which the first two seasons of Netflix's Castlevania are based on. Arguably, it's a better sequel to the show than the game; the art style and character designs have clear anime inspirations, and some voice actors reprise their roles. I enjoyed seeing them in a new light, especially as they passed the torch to Rose.

The best example of this torch-passing is a deck of tarot cards, gifted to Rose by her late mother, Sypha. It's my favorite mechanic in Belmont's Curse. Each is a specially crafted vessel to hold the power of Paris' most powerful figures, friend or foe. As you approach each target, Rose will gather clues from her surroundings to aid in hunting them down or besting them in battle. This investigation ranges from just-okay, linear moments in the early game to cryptic, map-spanning missions in the late game. Creatively designed quests to chase down the last handful of tarot cards were my favorite moments in the whole game.

Once you find your targets, you absorb their souls into a tarot card, allowing Rose to speak with them. What starts as a lonely journey becomes almost like a road trip, as Rose has a packed backseat of antagonists and allies who can chime in whenever they see fit.

Each card also unlocks a spell, ranging from fireballs and a magic cross to a blast turning foes to stone. They also come with a list of Works of Mercy: achievements that upgrade your spells upon completion. These range from "Use This Spell 20 Times" to "Kill 44 Skeletons" or "Find All The Rooms In The Clock Tower." It provides a fun reason to use abilities you might not otherwise, which is especially nice when you become overwhelmed by options in the game's closing hours. I was swayed on more than one spell after achievement-hunting for upgrades.

Speaking of combat, the action in Belmont's Curse is magnificent. Rose is light on her feet and only becomes lighter as she gains new abilities, becoming a whirlwind of death by the game's conclusion. Between six weapon categories, a variety of perk-granting relics, and your deck of cards, players are flooded with choice in how to approach encounters. I constantly switched up my loadout, often mid-fight, to adapt to the enemies that appeared. Fights were tough enough to keep me on my toes but never insurmountable, and a forgiving checkpoint system keeps repeated deaths from becoming frustrating. Inventive and intense boss battles against Dracula's coterie of minions are also a major highlight.

After 16 hours, I ended my playthrough with 92 percent completion. I just couldn't resist doubling back to earlier zones to see the new areas I could access with my newfound traversal abilities, and Belmont's Curse never failed to reward me. Between two dozen weapons to find, upgrades to health and mana, and even some huge, optional areas, I'm excited for the satisfaction that awaits me as I dive back in to reach 100 percent.

Belmont's Curse is a strong reminder of why the Castlevania series is so influential, with a rich world, tense combat, and a map chock-full of secrets. While this latest entry certainly pays homage to the Castlevanias of yore, it also breathes fresh life into the franchise, making me excited for its future. Castlevania is back, and between Rose Belmont and Evil Empire, it's in great hands.

#316. Heart Failure Is Becoming More Common in Adults Under 65, Report Finds

📅 Oct 12, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

An estimated 56 million people worldwide were living with heart failure in 2023, more than double the number in 1990, according to a new Global Burden of Disease (GBD) analysis published September 30 in JAMA Cardiology.

Heart failure is a chronic condition in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body’s needs. Despite its name, heart failure doesn’t mean the heart has stopped beating.

A separate 2026 report from the Heart Failure Society of America (HFSA) found that heart failure contributed to more than 450,000 deaths in the United States in 2023, with particularly sharp increases in mortality among adults under 65.

Approximately 7.7 million U.S. adults (3%) are living with heart failure, according to the HFSA report. That number is projected to rise to 8.7 million by 2030, 10.3 million by 2040, and 11.4 million by 2050.

The GBD researchers said the findings highlight the growing burden of heart failure associated with population growth and aging, as well as the need for prevention and treatment strategies targeting common causes such as ischemic heart disease and hypertension (high blood pressure).

Between 2010 and 2022, heart failure hospitalization rates increased among adults under 65 while declining among those 65 and older. Between 2001 and 2020, estimated heart failure mortality rates more than doubled among adults ages 25 to 44 and nearly doubled among those ages 45 to 64.

“I think we’re seeing the impact of cardiovascular risk factors developing earlier in life. Conditions such as obesity, diabetes, hypertension, and sleep apnea are affecting younger adults at higher rates than in previous generations,” Kevin Shah, MD, Program Director of Heart Failure Outreach at MemorialCare Heart & Vascular Institute at Long Beach Medical Center in Long Beach, CA, who wasn’t involved in the analysis, told Healthline.

“When those risk factors are present for decades, they can eventually lead to heart failure at a younger age,” he said.

Amit Blumfield, MD, a cardiologist at Manhattan Cardiology in New York City who was also not involved in the analysis, noted that improvements in early detection may also play a role.

“Our tools to detect early heart failure are also improving with time, and as a result, patients may receive an earlier diagnosis and can hopefully start medical therapy to prevent progression or possibly even reverse the process entirely.”

Heart failure contributed to 452,573 deaths in the United States in 2023, accounting for approximately 14.6% of all deaths that year, according to the HFSA report.

Age-adjusted heart failure mortality rates increased by approximately 37% between 2010 and 2023, with preliminary 2024 data suggesting the upward trend is continuing.

This increase appears to have reversed more than a decade of previous progress in reducing heart failure mortality.

Ischemic heart disease and hypertension were among the most common causes of heart failure in older adults, according to the GBD report. However, the underlying causes varied considerably by age group and region.

Among children and young adults, congenital heart abnormalities and rheumatic heart disease (RHD) were common causes of heart failure. Heart failure associated with alcohol use was most prevalent during middle adulthood.

#333. 12 Dishes and Drinks Everyone Should Try in Mexico City

📅 Oct 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Mexico City is a colossal, beautifully overwhelming metropolis and an absolute paradise for those who live to eat. Across thousands of restaurants, markets, street stalls, bakeries and fondas, food from every corner of the country converges. So deciding where (and what) to eat can feel daunting.

I’ve spent over a decade writing about Mexico’s food scene, along with popping up on TV shows to talk about it occasionally. My favorite way to navigate it all is still right here on the ground with my food tours. Sidestepping the obvious spots and encouraging travelers to go deeper into the food culture here constantly reminds me of the fascinating nature of how we chilangos eat.

To me, that means having the flexibility to move between completely different culinary worlds. We can spend lunchtime standing on a crowded sidewalk, leaning over a taco drenched in salsa and lime juice, then dress up that same evening for a multicourse dinner at a dimly lit restaurant. We’re fiercely loyal to the neighborhood places that have fed us for years and know our names, but we remain insatiably curious about the next opening or dish worth crossing town for.

That same flexibility shapes the city’s food. CDMX knows exactly how to pull tradition into modernity without losing its spirit. These 12 spots offer a taste of the city’s versatility: dishes and bites deeply rooted in history but shaped by the wonderfully chaotic, ever-evolving way we eat today.

Adolfo Prieto 1364 B, Tlacoquemecatl del Valle, Benito Juárez +52 55 5559 6358

I remember being dragged out of bed early on a weekend as a teenager to have breakfast here. Although I was still drowsy, the smell of the wood-fired stoves, the café de olla (traditional “pot coffee”) bubbling away, and sounds of the older musicians playing classic tunes on their guitars were enough to bring me around. I was fully awake by the time a plate of refried beans with scrambled eggs and a stack of warm tortillas made it to the table. It’s the kind of humble combination you’d find in almost any Mexican home. At Fonda Margarita, these eggs, which are cooked over a wood fire and enriched with lard, take on a different life. Getting here early is a requirement, as they open around 6:30 a.m. and by 10 a.m., all the food is gone.

C. Chilpancingo 35, Colonia Condesa, +52 55 5264 4976

Few breakfast menus are complete without chilaquiles. What started as a way to use up day-old tortillas is now one of the country’s quintessential breakfasts: triangles of tortillas, deep-fried until crisp, then bathed in salsa. At Chilpa in leafy Condesa, several salsas are available, from a red, tomato-based version to the bright, tangy tomatillo number, all with different spice levels. Pile on your choice of cecina, shredded chicken, or fried eggs. Crema, grated cheese, and chopped onion are standard, making it easy to order a different version every visit.

If you asked my Mexican grandma what breakfast looks like, she’d say coffee and a pan dulce—more often than not that would mean a concha. The quintessential Mexican pastry makes an appearance in virtually every bakery in town, from the most traditional versions made with a combination of lard and shortening to the tender butter-forward renditions found in most modern bakeries. At Panadería Gala, a speakeasy-style bakery, the extra-bouncy conchas get an extra layer of indulgence when topped with a white or dark chocolate ganache quenelle.

The Margarita may be known worldwide as the quintessential Mexican cocktail. Legend has it that the iconic drink was first conceived in Ensenada, Baja California, and its ties to coast and borderlands are still strong. But chilangos tend to save it for specific occasions and places such as a beach vacation, a favorite cantina, or a particular restaurant where we know they’ll make a killer one. At Despacho Margarita, the classic shaken version gets a new life, alongside spicy and frozen variations. In the slushy machine there are usually two flavors: a classic tequila and lime version and a seasonal mezcal-based one that changes monthly, with unique flavors such as nopal and shiso or raspberry and cayenne pepper.

Cantinas have long been places to gather over good food and several rounds of drinks. In these laid-back establishments, both are given equal importance. At Covadonga, a cantina without background music, generations mix at the tables, from the young and cool to sixty-somethings who come to play dominoes (available on request).

Banderas have been a popular order here from the beginning: three shot glasses lined up, one with tequila, another with lime juice, and a third with sangrita, a red-hued blend of tomato and fruit juices. Together they mimic the three colors of the Mexican flag (hence the name). They’re not meant to be knocked back but rather sipped slowly, alternating between glasses.

There’s a certain type of stand you’ll see repeatedly throughout the city centered around a large, round comal, usually fired with charcoal, with a bucket of corn masa and, more often than not, two women working side by side. Gorditas, quesadillas, and sopes are all on the menu, but the tlacoyo may be the most emblematic of central Mexico and Mexico City.

Football-shaped and stuffed with your choice of fava bean purée, requesón (a soft cheese similar to ricotta), or refried beans, tlacoyos are typically topped with cactus salad for a tangy contrast, grated queso fresco, and red or green salsa, depending on your preference. They're the kind of morning or afternoon snack you might grab during errands or before wandering into the adjacent market.

Petén 248 y, Avenida Universidad, Narvarte Poniente, Benito Juárez+52 55 5536 3060

The idea of stacking meat on a rotating spit was brought to Puebla by Lebanese immigrants. Once it reached Mexico City, it evolved into one of the city’s great late-night bites: the taco al pastor. At El Vilsito, a garage by day that moonlights as one of the city’s best taquerías, they take the pastor one step further, turning it into the most Chilango version possible by making it into a torta. A warm, lightly toasted telera gets a smear of refried beans, avocado, onion, tomato, and freshly shaved pastor, all finished with melted cheese.

Calle Versalles 113, Casa Versalles, Juárez+52 55 8568 7397

You can’t talk about Mexico City without talking about its obsession with seafood. It may seem strange for a city far from the coast, but seafood from both of Mexico’s coastlines makes its way here through the second-largest seafood market in the world (surpassed only by Tokyo’s Toyosu) before being distributed across the rest of the country.

At Taller de Ostiones, clams, barnacles, and tuna arrive three times a week by air from Baja California Sur, alongside sustainably farmed oysters. Those bivalves pair perfectly with a house-made sauce that’s rich, briny, deeply umami, and slightly spicy.

Mexico City runs on fondas and street food. These casual eateries serve homestyle food that’s quick, affordable, and feels like a warm hug. Stepping inside a fonda means being a part of a daily ritual known as comida corrida, where a meal typically unfolds in four acts: a steaming bowl of soup or broth to start, followed by your choice of rice or pasta. Parking rules are as important as driving rules, and although some vary from city to city, most are the same everywhere.

Driving around the country is a favorite American pastime, and I’ve certainly done my share. When I arrive in a city in which I’ve never been before, I like to ditch the car for a while and walk around. But that can be risky if you don’t know the parking rules that apply there. And given the traffic flow modifications that many cities have recently adopted, the rules (some of them, anyway) seem to be changing faster than drivers can keep up.

Where can you park and where can’t you? If parking is legal, how long can you stay without getting towed, and how much will it cost if you get towed? But really, there’s no mystery, according to auto insurance specialist Melanie Musson.  “Knowing where you can and cannot park is pretty easy if you pay attention.”  All you have to do, she says, is follow the signs.

You can always park in a municipal parking lot or parking garage, as long as there’s room and you pay the fee. It’s usually also OK to park on the street, especially if other cars are there, but if the space you choose has a meter, it’s important to feed it. Fines in congested urban areas can be expensive. The signs near an available parking space may be advising you of alternate street parking. That’s a parking rule that requires drivers to move their cars from one side of the street to the other on specific days and times so street sweepers can clean along the curb.
